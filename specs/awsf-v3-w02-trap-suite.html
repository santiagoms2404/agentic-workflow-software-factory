In Plain Language

This section explains the whole plan without technical vocabulary. Everything after it says the same things precisely, for the people and machines building it.

What this is about

The owner's software factory has stopped 47 real jobs so far. Some stopped for reasons nobody could have seen in advance. Many stopped for reasons that were visible before the job spent anything: a tool the job needed could not be found, the paid allowance was already used up, the job was asked to change a file it is not allowed to touch. A few of those reasons repeated, sometimes within hours, because nothing remembered the first one. The previous piece of work taught the factory to refuse a badly prepared job and made every new stop carry a written cause. Nothing yet turns a past stop into a lasting check, and the factory's health check does not look at the machine it runs on.

Why now

The next pieces of work let the factory do more without the owner watching: a deputy that answers routine questions overnight, a rule for which paid service runs which job, a new steering program. The owner decided that none of that freedom arrives until the factory refuses every mistake it has already made. This plan is that condition. Seven later pieces of work wait for it.

What we are building

Think of a flight school's simulator. Each time a real flight was aborted, an instructor recreates that flight's conditions in the simulator and checks that the aircraft's own systems now stop the take-off before the engines start. Each recreated scenario is kept and replayed on every new version of the aircraft. To be sure the scenario really tests something, the instructor also replays it once with the relevant warning light unplugged, and the scenario must then fail. Every new aborted flight goes into the log with either the name of its scenario or a written reason no scenario can catch it, such as a storm that arrived mid-flight. Alongside the simulator, the ground crew's checklist gains the questions behind the aborts that cannot be simulated: is the fuel truck here, does the pilot hold a valid licence, how much allowance is left, are old aircraft cluttering the hangar, and has finished paperwork been filed. The checklist only reports. It never fixes anything on its own.

How we will know it worked

Every past stop has been recreated and sorted: caught by a named check, or out of reach with a reason the owner agreed to. Every check refuses before anything is spent, and each one fails when its refusal is removed. Every stop after the agreed cut-off date names its check or its reason, and the factory says plainly when one does not. The health check names each machine fact it reads. Some things are deliberately not part of this: catching mistakes the factory has never made, stopping a job that runs out of allowance halfway through, choosing which paid service runs a job, and repairing anything the health check finds.

Problem

A stop teaches nothing durable today. Read on 2026-10-07 at e32e892:

  • Stops repeat. The forensics draft records the same rework defect stopping #12 and then #14 six hours later, and the same design-to-plan ENOENT stopping #21 and then #24 a day later. Since the draft, 4 of the 6 new blocks are quota-exhausted, and one of them (v3-w01-m5-k1#1) was refused by a Claude five-hour window that was already exhausted at its first build call. Nothing turns a stop into a check.
  • The quota pause exists and is off. production-run.ts probes quota at every phase boundary and pauses below routing.quota_stop, and the resume path refuses below it (around line 3815). awsf.config.yaml's routing block has no quota_stop, so this project never pauses, and nothing checks quota before the first phase reserves a call.
  • The launch environment is never checked. ClaudeCodeAdapter.isAvailable() validates the executable name and never resolves it (core/src/adapters/claude-code.ts:346–355), so #11 and #17 blocked with ExecutableNotFound "claude" after the run had started, while the owner's own terminal could run the same command. resolveExecutable in transport-broker.ts already does the search the broker will do.
  • A layer nobody runs lets a regression land. 07b20f9 (2026-10-01) made the protected execution proof require the resolved bwrap path the real broker records. The journeys' fake broker recorded a bare bwrap, so all 31 protected-grant journeys went BLOCKED, and the commit landed because this project's gates then ran test:unit, typecheck and lint only. 7bc48b5 fixed the fake the same day, and Q13 made journeys a landing gate on 2026-10-02. The lesson for traps is double: a trap that only passes is not evidence until something proves it can fail, and a layer that no gate runs protects nothing.
  • Doctor does not look at the machine. doctorCommand (core/src/cli/commands/doctor.ts, 105 lines) reports locks, recorded PIDs, recovery controllers, degraded sessions and the projection, plus W19's Jev row. It never reads Git storage, provider CLIs, logins, quota, branches, worktrees or plan markers. On 2026-10-02 the checkout held 22 local branches and 226 linked worktrees, 153 under the factory root. It has no repair path, by design, and keeps none.
  • No cause names a trap. awsf.attribution/v1 carries cause and reason only (core/src/contracts/attribution-record.ts). Four attribution records exist on the owner's state root: one factory (2026-09-29) and three owner on cancels since M1 landed. None of the 6 blocks since the draft has one.

The trap, defined

A trap is four things that agree
(1) A catalogue entry in core/src/traps/catalogue.ts: id TR-NN, family, title, the seed stops it covers, and where it refuses. (2) A trap test in core/test/traps/ that rebuilds the seed's preconditions in a synthetic repository and state root, drives the production CLI or runner on the stub adapter, and asserts the named refusal with zero calls reserved, zero adapter invocations and the attempt still DRAFT or PREPARED. (3) One refusal marker in core/src, a // trap-refusal-begin TR-NN … // trap-refusal-end TR-NN pair around the code that refuses. (4) A mutant: the harness copies the source to a scratch directory, deletes the marked block, runs the trap against the copy, and requires it to fail. A trap without a passing mutant is not counted.

A seed that cannot have a trap gets a no-trap entry instead: the seed, one reason kind and its evidence. The kinds are closed:

Reason kindMeansExample seed
fixedThe stop came from a factory defect that has been removed. Cites the fixing commit and the regression test that keeps it removed.#5 InvalidReviewInversion, fixed in 1aafbe5
after-spendThe condition only arises after provider calls began: model output, a mid-run quota exhaustion, a provider outage during review. Names any mitigation, such as the phase-boundary pause.#10 planner emitted no valid envelope
ownerAn owner decision, such as cancelling to replace a task.v3-w01-m5-t13#1
unexplainedNo recorded evidence names a cause, typically a cancel from before M1 made reasons mandatory.#13, cancelled with no reason
not-a-stopFor live gotchas only: surprising behaviour that never stops a run.gotcha 6, awsf gc never deletes

The population and the cut

A stop is an attempt, in any registered project's state root, whose lifecycle state is BLOCKED or CANCELLED and whose workflow is not prove. Prove replays are the proving ground: 99 CANCELLED and 1 BLOCKED on 2026-10-07, created by owner acts at pinned bases to replay recorded requests. This answers handoff C2: the JSON cause ledger includes them, and the trap population never does.

Execution, owner gates and fences

Managed by default (decision 7). Each ticket runs as one build-review attempt, so a provider other than the builder's reviews it. awsf new derives the tier. K1 binds every start: the driver runs awsf preflight, the owner runs awsf confirm, then awsf start. External prerequisites must have landed. In an owner-approved accumulating shift, a selected predecessor is satisfied by its host-committed, gate-passing head in the same managed worktree; it needs neither a separate landing nor a marker change mid-shift. No ticket touches a protected path, so any milestone may run as one shift where the sizing fits. Manual execution is the logged exception, for a factory that cannot carry the work.

A red trap at the base stops the factory. Once G02-L makes traps a gate, K1's suite field requires it green at every base. A trap that turns red on main therefore refuses every start, the fix included, and that fix is a direct session logged as a decision-7 exception, as W01's R3 already provides. No bypass flag is built for it (INV-7). The coverage signal of W02-Q2 is kept out of every gate for the same reason.

What these mechanisms do not cover

  • A trap refuses a known shape. A stop of a new shape is unprotected until its trap lands, and a stop that arises after spend is never refused.
  • The mutant proves the trap depends on the marked block. It does not prove the block is the only refusal, or that the trap's preconditions match the real stop's.
  • The coverage fence covers stop codes, not causes: phase-abort spans many shapes, so one covering entry does not mean every phase-abort is trapped.
  • Doctor reports what it reads at that moment. A login can lapse and a window can close between doctor and the run, which is why task 9 checks at run start.
  • The launch-environment check resolves the executable. It does not prove the executable starts, is authenticated, or is the version the adapter expects.

Rejected while authoring

  • A test that fails on an unlinked stop. It must read runtime state, and red, it would freeze K1 for every start (W02-Q2).
  • A refusal switch for mutants. Any caller could reach it (W02-Q4, INV-7).
  • Traps for after-spend stops. A check after the first call is a mitigation; calling it a trap would let GR open on stops that still spend.
  • Doctor that prunes worktrees. The prompt forbids a repair path, and invariant 8 forbids an auto-delete path.
  • Folding the trap layer into journeys by default. Q2 decided a separate layer; it stays G02-L's stated alternative.
  • Spawning provider CLIs from doctor. W02-Q8.
