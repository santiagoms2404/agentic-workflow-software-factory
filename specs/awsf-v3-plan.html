Plan: AWSF v3 — The Spine

v1 built a factory that can build this repository. v2 made it a factory the owner can point at any of their projects. v3 makes it a factory that drives itself between the owner's decisions: the procedure is code, judgment runs on the owner's subscriptions, every stop is explained, and every landing is the owner's.

This is a spine plan. Each of its fifteen workstreams gets its own deep plan before any of its code is written. This document holds the workstream list, the phases, the dependencies, the shared invariants, the amendments and the open Questionables. Authoring it wrote no code, called no provider, created no worktree, changed no invariant and moved no marker.

In Plain Language

This section explains the whole plan without technical vocabulary. Everything after it says the same things precisely, for the people and machines building it.

What this is about

The owner has built a software factory: a set of software assistants that plan, write, check and hand over changes to the owner's projects, with the owner approving every change that goes in. It is good at checking work and at staying inside its rules. It is poor at running on its own. Only about 8 of every 100 changes to the factory's own code went through the factory. Every finished change still needs the owner at least twice. The assistant that steers the factory keeps its procedure in its head, forgets steps, and rereads a very large amount of text on every turn. Of 27 jobs that stopped, 8 stopped for reasons a simple checklist would have caught, and almost no stop has a written reason.

Why now

The second version of the factory has reached what it set out to do. What remains of it was planned before the owner decided to run everything on the subscriptions they already pay for, before a dedicated steering program was on the table, and before a local model was. Two old rules also need restating: the factory may now choose which model runs a job, within limits the owner sets, and it may run a program the owner starts and watches, but never one that runs unattended. A new laptop is coming, and the owner's priority project, a health app, needs readiness checks and a test release channel. Writing the third version's map now keeps the remaining work pointed at one goal.

What we are building

Think of the factory as a kitchen and the steering assistant as a head chef who works from memory. This plan moves the recipes onto the wall. First, the kitchen gets a checklist at the door that refuses an order missing an ingredient, a binder of every past mistake that is tested daily, a way to cross dishes off the menu once they are served, and a recording of every shift. Only after the binder refuses every known mistake does the kitchen get more freedom: a deputy who may handle routine calls while the owner is away, a rule for which cook takes which order, a taster who tries each dish the way a customer would, and a new head chef who reads the wall instead of remembering it. Then the kitchen learns: it keeps a short index instead of rereading every cookbook, trains a small local cook on its own records, and checks how well each project's kitchen is laid out. Last, it moves to the new building, sends dishes to a tasting room before they reach customers, listens to how they were received, and gets a desk app and phone alerts. The owner still signs off every dish that leaves.

How we will know it worked

At least half of the factory's own changes go through the factory, up from 8 in 100. At least 7 of 10 jobs finish and are accepted, up from 1 in 4. Each accepted change needs the owner once. No job stops for a known, checkable mistake. Every stop and every cancellation says why. The steering program rereads less than a twentieth of what it rereads today. Every request the owner makes is captured. A taster checks behaviour before the owner does. A small local model has been compared fairly against the paid one on the same tests. The health app has readiness checks and a test release channel. Some things are deliberately not part of this: the factory never approves its own changes, never releases to real users without the owner, never runs unattended in the background, never learns from the paid models' own writing, and is not built for other people to use.

Purpose

Move the driving procedure from a model's memory into the host, so the factory runs between the owner's decisions and each of those decisions is one act. The line v1 and v2 drew holds unchanged: the host advances lifecycle state, models supply structured facts, and a human authorises anything that lands. v3 extends that line to the driver. The ceiling is the intent's eight items, measured by the acceptance targets below, for AWSF and for Smart Health.

Problem

The procedure lives in the driver's memory, and that is where it fails. The assessment graded AWSF 53/100 against the three pillars of a software factory. Verification and governance score near the top. What holds the grade down is autonomy, and every autonomy gap traces back to the driver.

  • The driver skips checks. In a draft review of 27 blocked runs, 8 were stops a mechanical check would have refused: a red base, a write boundary that missed a named path, a missing grant, storage on the Windows mount. The checks exist only as prose the driver must remember.
  • Stops are unexplained. 1 of 36 stops carries a recorded cause. 9 cancels carry none, because awsf cancel records the fixed detail "explicit CLI cancellation" (core/src/cli/commands/cancel.ts:98) and the attribution vocabulary has no driver cause (core/src/contracts/attribution-record.ts:15).
  • The legal next step is prose. LEGAL_EDGES in core/src/state/task-machine.ts knows every legal move and who may make it, while the CLI prints a prose nextAction the driver must interpret.
  • The guard has a spelling gap. The owner-act matcher catches awsf land but not npm run awsf --silent -- land, the documented spelling (F17). The lifecycle matcher in the same file already reads the npm form, so the gap is known and narrow.
  • Gates do not cover every layer. Commit 07b20f9 broke 31 protected-grant journeys and landed anyway, because awsf.project.yaml gates run unit tests, typecheck and lint, never the journeys layer. 7bc48b5 fixed the fake broker the same day.
  • Plans drift from code. No role can write specs/*.html, so no run closes its own markers. W18 M5's code is on main while its markers still say open.
  • The driver is the most expensive part. A driver turn re-sends a median of 168k prompt tokens. 41% of owner asks are captured.
  • The owner is the only black-box tester, and validation is 20% of phase time against the talk's 37%.

Solution

Four driving layers, each owning what it is best at. L1 is AWSF code: the lifecycle, preflight refusals and the legal next steps. L2 is decisions: W19's decision service picks among legal steps at routine stops. L3 is the conversational driver: it talks with the owner, captures asks and fills command arguments, with Sol first and a local fine-tune later if it earns the role. L4 is frontier models through their official CLIs, for planning and diagnosis.

Five driver checks hold the layers to that split. K1: the DRAFT→PREPARED step refuses without a driver preflight record. K2: awsf next --json returns the legal next steps and who may take each. K3: the driver's output must match a schema of legal driver steps only. K4: read-only actions run freely, one confirmation card covers new, start and run, and owner acts are only ever prepared. K5: the trap suite. K1, K2 and K5 are Phase A. K3 and K4 arrive with the harness in Phase B.

Reliability comes before autonomy. Phase A makes skipped checks impossible and records everything. Phase B widens what runs without the owner, inside the owner's subscriptions. Phase C makes the factory cheaper and smarter from its own data. Phase D closes the loop from staging release back to intake and reaches the Mac and the phone.

Shared Invariants and Constraints

The thirteen invariants hold unless an amendment lands first

AGENTS.md's invariants bind every workstream. Several are enforced by meta-tests, so a deep plan that assumes otherwise fails its own gates. The ones v3 presses hardest: 1 (no live task state in any committed file), 2 (markers are earned), 3 and 4 (one child-process module, no shell), 5 (pure state), 6 (SQLite written only by the projector, sqlite.ts and migrations), 7 (the dependency allowlist), 8 (no push before LANDED, no force, no auto-delete), 11 (no agent identity in any commit), 12 (a plan and its tickets never disagree) and 13 (the Delegate acts only under an owner lease, and never as the owner).

Decisions already taken

The intent's fourteen decisions are constraints, not questions. The ones that shape this spine most: subscriptions first, with API and paid compute as budgeted exceptions (1); no frontier output in training data (2); the MacBook Pro M5 Pro with 64 GB as the planning baseline, AWSF in a Linux VM there, WSL until it arrives (3); landing human-only through v3 (4); routing as an owner-approved policy, superseding v2 intent decision 5 (5); no unattended daemon (6); the factory builds AWSF by default with direct sessions as a logged exception (7); a light planning tier, closed v2 specs out of the priming path (8); the harness's surface, confirmation policy and intents (9); scripted validator checks first (10); W19 rebased with M5→W07, M6–M7→W09, M8→W10 (11); a staging channel for Smart Health with production as an owner act (12); reliability before autonomy (13); no model emits an owner act (14).

Gates this spine cannot resequence

GR: the reliability gate (decision 13)
No workstream that widens what runs without the owner lands any of that widening before W02 is [x], which means its trap suite refuses every known trap. The workstreams it binds are W05, W06, W07 (its agent-driven half), W08, W10, W13 and W14. Each lists W02 in depends_on, so the sync fence refuses a done ticket for any of them while W02 is open. The fence enforces completion order. The landing-level rule is stricter, and each of those deep plans carries GR as a gate in front of its first widening build.
GA: the amendment gate
Every invariant or protected-path change is proposed one at a time, names what it blocks with evidence, the replacement guarantee in a still-checkable form, its cost and the alternative that avoids it, and lands as an owner-authored commit before the build that depends on it. No workstream invents a mechanism that lets an agent write AGENTS.md, awsf.config.yaml, awsf.project.yaml or any other protected path.
GM: deep plans are authored manually until W03 lands
Decision 7 makes managed execution the default for deep plans, but no factory role can write specs/*.html today (the v2 W15 finding, still true at b5e8ffa). So every deep-plan authoring session in this spine is a manual, logged exception until W03 lands a closure path. Each authoring session records itself as a direct-session exception under Q11's rule (decided 2026-10-02). Implementation work inside each deep plan defaults to the factory.
