In Plain Language

This section explains the whole plan without technical vocabulary. Everything after it says the same things precisely, for the people — and machines — building it.

A promise made at the start, and nobody checking it was kept

Before this workshop builds anything, it writes down what the finished work has to do. Then it breaks that into a list of jobs, and hands the jobs out one at a time. There is already a strict clerk who checks that the list of jobs and the master document agree — if a job is quietly deleted, renamed, or marked finished when it is not, the clerk stops everything and says so.

The clerk is good at their job and is checking the wrong thing. They compare the job list against the job list. Nobody compares either one against the promises. So a promise made at the beginning — "the finished thing must never do this", "you will know it works when you can see that" — can be dropped on the way to the job list, and every check still passes. The promise was written in ordinary sentences, and ordinary sentences are not something a clerk can tick off.

Why now, and not later

Two things just became true. The workshop learned to keep several projects apart and to know, for any master document, exactly which job list belongs to it — so there is finally a reliable place to put a promise and a reliable way to find it again. And the workshop is about to start using this same start-to-finish routine for real work rather than for practice. Adding the missing check now costs a few days. Adding it after a year of documents means going back through all of them.

There is also a small thing that has to happen before a bigger thing, and getting the order wrong is expensive. A separate piece of work is about to tidy up how the workshop writes instructions for its assistants — today the same instructions are assembled in three different places. This plan introduces two new kinds of assistant. If it did that first, there would be four places to tidy instead of three. So the new assistants are the very last thing this plan builds, and everything else is arranged to go first.

Give every promise a number, and make the numbers travel

Think of a building inspection. Before anything is poured, the architect writes a short numbered list: things that must always be true, and things you should be able to see when it is finished. Every item gets a number. From then on, every work order on that site cites the numbers it exists to satisfy. At the end, an inspector does something very simple: walk the numbered list, and check that each number appears on at least one work order. A promise nobody took responsibility for is now visible in about a second, without reading anything.

That is the whole idea. The numbered promises get written once, at the design stage, and everything downstream carries them rather than rewriting them — which matters, because a number copied by hand is a number that can be copied wrong. Here the workshop itself carries them forward, so the only thing anyone has to decide is which job serves which promise.

Between the design and the job list sits a second reader who did not write the design and is asked one question: what is wrong with this? Their answer is written down as a form with numbered boxes, not as a verdict that moves the work along by itself. That distinction is the difference between an opinion and a record. The form is kept; a simple, unarguable rule reads it; and if the form lists anything serious, the workshop does not go on to the expensive next step. It stops and waits for a person.

And one rule about that stop, because it is easy to get backwards: the workshop never asks the second reader to look again at an unchanged design. Asking someone to re-examine something that has not changed, when their previous answer was inconvenient, is asking them to change their mind. The design is what has to change, and the person who wrote it has already left the room.

How anyone would know it worked

Three things become observably true, and one thing deliberately does not.

  • A deleted promise turns the checks red. Not "should" — the plan is not finished until someone has deliberately deleted one, watched everything go red, put it back, and written down the exact words of the complaint. A check nobody has seen fail is decoration.
  • Nothing in the routine depends on an assistant having read a separate handbook first. Some of the workshop's assistants are hired through an agency that does not let them bring handbooks. The routine is therefore built so that no step ever needed one.
  • The master document, the instructions, and the job list are produced together, from one source, by the workshop itself. Two documents that are supposed to say the same thing are now the same sentence written out twice by one machine, rather than two copies somebody has to keep matched.
  • What is deliberately not claimed: when the second reader's form comes back with nothing serious on it, that means the form is filled in correctly. It does not mean the design is good, and it never will. That sentence is printed on the form itself, next to the result, so nobody has to remember it — and the reader is required to say what they did not look at, because a review that claims to have checked everything has told you nothing.

One more thing, and it is the piece that decides whether any of this is useful. The real project this is being built for keeps its written plans in one folder and its actual code in two others. So before the first reader starts, the workshop looks up all three, writes down exactly which version of each it found, and hands that list over. The readers may look at everything; the workshop only ever writes into the plans folder. And if one of the three cannot be found, everything stops right there — because a design written against two out of three looks finished, and that is the expensive kind of mistake.

Worth saying plainly: an earlier draft of this plan claimed the readers were not allowed to look at the other two folders. That turned out to be wrong — nothing had ever stopped them, and nobody had checked. Finding out cost three extra pieces of work and no money, because the plan was still only a plan.

Purpose

Make a plan's claims checkable by the same machinery that already checks its tickets. A design states what must stay true and what must become true, gives each of those statements an identifier, and the identifier travels — into the plan, into the tickets, and into a test that fails when one of them is dropped. Between the design and the plan sits an independent architecture review whose verdict is a typed envelope rather than a state change, and a deterministic check that refuses to buy the planning call while the review still reports a blocker.

Every stage is a compiled recipe with a typed envelope. Nothing in the chain depends on an installed skill having been reachable, and nothing in it depends on a document having been read.

Problem

AWSF can already run a plan-shaped workflow, and the plan it produces is prose. A planning envelope carries implementationSteps[].acceptanceCriteria as an array of free-text strings, and nothing downstream ever reads them again. The ticket set that a plan spawns is checked hard — coverage, milestone grouping, state, byte-identical prompts, dependency order — by core/test/unit/meta/ticket-plan-sync.test.ts, and none of those assertions can see whether the work described actually serves the design that motivated it. A task can be deleted from a plan and its acceptance criterion vanishes with it, silently.

The route the source record proposed cannot run

The greenfield planning candidate described "a plan recipe emitting a planf3 HTML" — a workflow phase that invokes an installed skill. It cannot exist. core/src/adapters/pi-codex.ts:621 launches every pi child with --no-skills, in a block that also passes --no-extensions, --no-prompt-templates, --no-themes and --no-context-files. A phase on that route reaches no installed skill, no prompt template, and no context file.

And the two routes disagree, which is worse than either of them refusing. The Claude adapter passes no skill-disabling flag at all; what closes the door there is its tool allowlist — --tools Read,Glob,Grep for the read-only profile — and the eight-name tool map at core/src/adapters/claude-code.ts:139-147, which has no member that invokes a skill. So the door is shut on both routes, by two different mechanisms. Since routing.review: invert-provider and routing.no_fallback: true mean a phase's provider is resolved at run time rather than at authoring time, a design that leaned on a skill would not merely be unavailable — it would be unavailable differently depending on which provider the phase landed on. That is the concrete form of the rule that no gate may depend on a document having been read.

The review that becomes a transition stops being a review

core/src/state/task-machine.ts carries ten task states and exactly twenty-five legal edges, and its own header says why PLANNING/BUILDING/DOCUMENTING are deliberately absent: "they are phases, and promoting them to states would make this matrix workflow-dependent and destroy its testability." An architecture review that earned its own edge would do exactly that. It would also need a write to core/src/state/**, which is a protected path no agent can write and a purity-fenced directory — so the design that needs a new edge needs an owner amendment for a thing the matrix already refuses on principle.

The zero-blocker count is a shape check that reads like a quality claim

A review reporting no blocking findings proves that the review's envelope is internally consistent. It proves nothing about how hard the reviewer looked. Every deployment of a counted verdict eventually gets read as "the design is clean", and the moment it does, the count starts being optimised rather than measured. This plan treats that as a defect to be designed against rather than a caveat to be footnoted.

Solution

One recipe, design-to-plan, with seven phases and three agent calls. Five new envelope contracts, five new gates, one grammar extension to the plan format W04 already declares and parses, one host renderer, and one extension to invariant 12's meta-test. Two new agent roles. No new task state, no new legal edge, no new CLI command.

What the distinct schema id costs, and it is not free

The same discrimination that keeps the architecture review out of the transition machinery keeps it out of automatic provider inversion. production-run.ts finds the phase to invert with compiled.phases.find(isReviewPhase) — the same schema-id test — and then requires the configured reviewer route to resolve to the opposite provider from the worker. A phase carrying awsf.architecture-review-output/v1 is invisible to that block, so routing.review: invert-provider does nothing for it.

What survives, and it is the property that matters here. Independence for an architecture review is a fresh session that did not write the design, and that holds unconditionally: both roles carry continuity: none, so the reviewer inherits none of the designer's turns whatever provider it lands on. Cross-provider difference is the stronger property the code reviewer gets, because a review of a diff benefits from a second model's blind spots.

So this plan buys it with configuration rather than with a mechanism: the amendment puts architecture-reviewer on the adapter designer is not on. That is weaker than the code reviewer's guarantee — it is a convention a later config edit can undo silently, where the reviewer's is enforced by InvalidReviewInversion at route resolution. The alternative was refused deliberately: extending the inversion test to a second schema id would put the architecture review back inside the machinery that also selects the L11 spawn site, and the whole design rests on it staying out. The named upgrade is to separate those two questions in production-run.ts — which phase inverts, and which phase is a task-machine transition — so a phase can answer yes to the first and no to the second. That belongs to the workstream that owns the composition site, not to this one.

The identifier spine, end to end

Two consequences fall out of that, and both are worth naming because they turn assertions into structure:

  • Transcription drift is impossible, not merely detected. The planner never restates an identifier set; it references one the host composed. A gate that had to catch a mistyped AC-3 is replaced by an arrangement in which there is nothing to mistype.
  • Invariant 12's byte-identity clause becomes true by construction for anything this chain produces. A ticket's build prompt and its § Section B block are the same string, written twice by one function in one commit. The existing meta-test stops being a discipline check and becomes a regression check.

Reading three repositories, writing one

The project this factory exists to build keeps its plans in one repository and its code in two others. A chain that could only reason about the repository it runs in would be the wrong deliverable, so the chain reads every repository the project declares and writes only into the one it runs in.

The permission this needs does not exist, because nothing was ever forbidden. path-policy judges write targets only — its three violation reasons are invalid-path, outside-write-globs and protected-path. And core/src/policy/sandbox-broker.ts binds --ro-bind / / before binding the worktree writable over it, with the comment saying so outright: "the whole host filesystem is readable above; writes: [] confines WRITES and confines reads not at all." A read-only phase can already reach a second repository. What was missing is that nothing told it where to look and nothing recorded what was available.

The reproducibility unit is a revision, not a byte — and that is weaker than the code reviewer's

review_evidence_present proves the exact bytes of a diff reached the model, by digesting the host-private copy on disk. design_evidence_present cannot make that claim and does not try. That is the right unit rather than a shortcut. A reviewer judges a fixed diff, so the diff is the evidence. The residual, stated rather than hidden: another process may commit to smart-health-api mid-attempt, and the recorded revision is a record of what was there, not a lock on it.

Why the zero-blocker gate must not be correctable, stated once and then enforced

Every other gate in this chain fails because the party who can fix it is still in the session. The zero-blocker gate is different: the party at fault is the designer, and the designer's session closed two phases ago. A correction round here would resume the reviewer and ask it to try again against an unchanged design — which is asking it to produce a different verdict about the same artifact. That is an incentive to soften a finding, and a tersely agreeable reviewer is precisely the rubber-stamped closure the independent review exists to prevent.

So the check does not live in a phase's gate list at all. It lives on the host plan-context phase, which composes the planner's input. A review that reports a blocker cannot produce a valid plan context, the plan phase's call is never reserved, and the attempt stops at AWAITING_OWNER with the findings in the journal. The owner may still continue — L19 is a legal edge — but it is an interactive human act, recorded with actor human, and it draws on a correction allowance. An override that costs something and is written down is the right shape; an override a gate can be talked into is not.

The limit belongs on the green row, not in a footnote

GateReport records a note on every check it performs, passing or failing — its own comment says "a passing report still explains every check it performed", and those notes are persisted and projected into the dashboard. So the limit is written into the passing note itself:

report.check(
  "no blocking findings",
  blocking.length === 0,
  `${blocking.length} blocking of ${findings.length} finding(s); this gate ` +
  "checks the verdict's shape, not the review's thoroughness — a zero here " +
  "is envelope consistency, never a clean design",
);

A unit test asserts that sentence is present in the passing report, so it cannot be quietly trimmed to make output tidier. That is what "put it where a reader of a green gate will see it" means mechanically: the limit travels with the row, into the journal, into the projection, and into anything that renders a gate result.

The envelope carries a second, independent statement of the same limit: limitations is required to be non-empty, so a review that claims it checked everything is refused. The count says how many blockers were found; the limitations say where the reviewer did not look. Neither is derivable from the other.

The grammar extension, and why the format id does not move

W04 froze one plan grammar — awsf-plan-html/v1, declared in awsf.project.yaml and parsed by parseAwsfPlanHtmlV1, which refuses foreign heading forms by name rather than parsing them to zero tasks. The identifier spine extends it in the same idiom the format already uses for machine-readable markers, a class-tagged <code>:

<!-- declaration, in the plan's own spine section -->
<dt><code class="inv">INV-1</code></dt>
<dd>core/src/state/** stays free of I/O.</dd>
<dt><code class="ac">AC-2</code></dt>
<dd>A dropped acceptance criterion turns the coverage gate red.</dd>

<!-- claim, inside a task's block -->
<p class="serves"><code class="serves">AC-2</code> <code class="serves">awsf-v2-plan#INV-1</code></p>

The extension is additive and optional, so the format id stays at /v1. A plan that declares no identifiers is a legitimate shape and is not failed — the same accounting the fence already makes for tier and workflow, whose own comment reads "present-but-wrong is the defect; absent is a legitimate shape." Bumping the format instead would force every plan already in specs/ to be re-declared for no gain. The named trigger for a bump is making the spine mandatory, which is a decision this workstream deliberately does not take.

What this does not do to plan-sota

The owner's hand-authoring route is untouched. Both routes emit the same declared format, so a plan the factory renders and a plan the owner writes are read by the same parser, joined by the same coverage gate, and paired with their tickets by the same registry lookup. One format, two authors. The planf3 → plan-sota rename is owner-side migration work, executed on 2026-08-20, and it does not re-enter this or any task graph.

Q5, decided 2026-08-21 — identifiers are unique per plan

AC/INV ids are unique per plan, and a cross-plan reference is written <plan-stem>#INV-n. The reasoning, so this plan can defend it rather than merely obey it: the identifier is what the coverage gate joins on, and the fence being extended — ticket-plan-sync — is already per plan set. Giving the new join the same scope as the machinery underneath it leaves one scope to reason about instead of two. Both wider options need an allocator handing out the next free id across several plans and repositories, which is shared mutable state; this repository has refused that consistently, and TicketStore's own comment says there is "intentionally no database, cache, or index."

The collision, already resolved — depend on it, do not rediscover it

The architecture review needs a fresh subagent that did not write the design. marimba's guard denies delegation-shaped tools. W01 settled this and its contract states the exception in a form this plan cites verbatim — docs/driving/marimba/CONTRACT.md §5:

"The guard is delivered per invocation, so a session launched without marimba's settings keeps the delegation tools, and that is the supported route for a review by an agent that did not write the proposal. Such a session is reviewing, not driving: it reads, judges and reports, and it takes no owner act."

Nothing in this workstream re-examines that, and nothing in it changes the guard. The runtime chain does not need the exception at all — the architecture-review phase is an ordinary governed phase launched by the host, not a delegation from a driving session. The exception matters for the other use of an architecture review: an owner-driven review of a design that has not become an attempt yet. Both are supported; only the second touches the guard.
