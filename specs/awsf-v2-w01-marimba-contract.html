In Plain Language

This section explains the whole plan without technical vocabulary. Everything after it says the same things precisely, for the people — and machines — building it.

A helper runs the workshop, and nobody has written down what it may do

There is a workshop that builds software, and there is a helper who operates it: choosing what to work on, starting the machines, watching, and reporting back. The helper is not supposed to do certain things — hand work to other helpers who are not on the books, or take the few final decisions that belong to the owner alone. Until now that was an understanding rather than a written rule, and the few notes that did exist described the wrong thing as the safeguard. They said the safeguard was that those final decisions can only be taken by someone typing at a keyboard. That is not a safeguard. It is a check on what the room looks like, and it is easy to make a room look right.

Why now

This helper is about to be trusted to write thirteen more plans, each of which decides how a piece of the workshop gets built. A helper whose limits are written down wrongly will pass that wrong description along thirteen times. Fixing it costs a few days and no new machinery, and it is the cheapest thing in the whole programme.

What we are building

Think of a doorman posted at the workshop's door. Every time the helper reaches for something, the doorman looks at what is being reached for and turns away two kinds of request: handing work to an unbooked helper, and the owner's own final decisions. The doorman already exists and already works. What this plan adds is three things. A short rule sheet the helper is handed at the start of every shift, which says plainly what it may do, what the doorman turns away, and — this is the part everyone skips — exactly which ways past the doorman are still open. A set of rehearsals that check the doorman by presenting him with two dozen prepared requests and confirming which he waves through and which he turns back, all of which can be run at any time without calling anyone or spending anything. And repairs to the three existing notes that describe the old, wrong safeguard, so the workshop does not carry two answers to the same question.

How we know it worked

Someone who has never seen this can run one command and watch the rehearsals pass, including a rehearsal that deliberately presents a request the doorman should wave through, so a doorman who turned everything away could not pass by being useless. The rule sheet will be short enough to read in three minutes, will name no command, and will state its own gaps out loud. The three wrong notes will be gone, and a check will fail loudly if that wording ever comes back. Deliberately not part of this: making the doorman impossible to get past. He cannot be, the plan says so in the rule sheet, and pretending otherwise is the exact mistake this work exists to undo.

Purpose

Give the driving role a written operating contract, delivered as an appended system prompt on every marimba invocation, that states its boundary as what that boundary actually is — a denial at the tool surface — and names, with measured evidence, everything that denial does not cover. Land the guard that performs the denial inside this repository as its single source of truth, with an offline test matrix that proves both of its fences bite and that its exclusions still pass. Repair the three documents already in docs/driving/ that describe a terminal-shape check as an authorisation boundary, because a new contract that contradicts them would give the tree two answers to one question.

Scope discipline. Installing the guard is an owner act and is not a task here. Extending the guard's coverage is not in scope either; three candidate hardenings surfaced while measuring it, and all three are Questionables rather than tasks, because the spine's scope for W01 is the contract plus the guard's tests and captured refusals.

Problem

Three claims about this boundary are false, and two of them are not merely in an old candidate record — they are live in the driving tree today, in the documents a driving session is told to read. A fourth problem is structural: the guard that makes the boundary real lives at a per-user path outside this repository, so nothing in the suite can see it, and its behaviour is currently asserted by four hand-captured anecdotes rather than by a test that runs on demand.

A fifth, discovered while measuring: the guard's stated limits are narrower than its real ones

The guard's header names one fail-open case — malformed JSON. Measured 2026-08-21, there are more, and one of them is silent. The full table is in What Was Measured; the short version is that the guard parses its payload with python3, and a python3 that is missing or broken disables both fences and exits 0, with nothing announcing it. On a machine where python3 is absent, marimba runs with no boundary and no signal.

Solution

Four artifacts, one repair pass, and one owner amendment. The contract is a short document that travels with every marimba invocation as appended system-prompt text; the guard becomes a committed file that the owner's settings point at, so there is exactly one copy of it; the tests are an offline payload matrix plus a set of document-level fences; and the tree's three wrong sentences are replaced with the measured statement and pinned by a fence so the wording cannot come back.

Why the contract is command-free, route-free and short

The spine fixes "command-free"; the other two properties are derived, and their reasons are worth stating because they are what makes the route collision resolvable at all.

  • Route-free, because the contract travels and the tree may not. A cookbook is read on demand from a checkout that is present. The contract is injected as system-prompt text into a session that may be driving a different project entirely — which is precisely what W04's registry makes routine. A relative path inside it would be an instruction to read a file that is not there. So the contract cites nothing by relative path, and a fence asserts it. That is the honest resolution of the route collision: not a test dodge, but a property the artifact needs for its own reasons.
  • Short, because it is paid on every request. A route is read once when it is needed; an appended system prompt is re-sent on every turn of every marimba session for as long as the session lives. The budget is ≤ 120 lines and ≤ 1,100 words, checked mechanically. This is also why the contract restates none of the tree: it states the rule, the guard enforces the list, and the cookbook explains the choice — three layers, one source each.
  • Command-free, mechanically defined. No fenced shell block, and no backticked awsf <verb>, just <target> or npm run <script> token. The contract therefore names the owner acts by their property — the acts the lifecycle reserves for the owner — and never by list. The list has one source: the six commands in core/src/cli/main.ts that construct an owner terminal.

What the contract must say, in eight parts

  1. What the driving role is, and the one line that separates it from worker work.
  2. The boundary, stated as a denial at the tool surface performed by a hook delivered per invocation, with two fences: delegation-shaped tool names, and owner-act command text.
  3. What the boundary does not cover — seven measured items, each stated plainly. This is the section the contract exists for.
  4. The measured settings semantics: --settings merges, --setting-sources excludes, and a project hook returning allow does not override marimba's deny.
  5. The scope exception W05 depends on: the guard is per invocation, so a review session launched without marimba's settings keeps the delegation tools.
  6. The four guarantees that replace the retired claims: no push path exists, nothing is auto-deleted, writes are confined to a managed worktree by path-policy, and canonical movement happens only through a human-approved local fast-forward.
  7. What marimba does instead of an owner act: prepare and explain. One line; the cookbook already holds the detail.
  8. Invariant 1 applied to the contract's own text: no live task state, ever, including in its examples.

The three rows that changed this plan

  1. Indirection evades fence 2. This is the honest ceiling of the whole design and it belongs in the contract's own text, not in a footnote. The boundary holds against mistake, drift and the direct shape. It does not hold against a session that decides to route around it, and no arrangement of substring matching will change that. Sharpened while building task 4, measured the same day against the committed script: the shell variable is one member of a class rather than the gap itself. A line continuation between the command and the verb, a quoted verb (awsf "land" T), a verb split across a quote (awsf l"and" T) and a backslash before one of its letters (awsf la\nd T) all exit 0 — while a tab exits 2, because " ".join(c.split()) collapses it. Whitespace is the only shell transformation the parser undoes; every other one is written text the match does not contain. That is what makes the class unbounded rather than enumerable, and it is why the guard states the ceiling instead of closing it and why task 4's matrix pins one representative rather than four.
  2. A broken interpreter disables the guard silently. Combined with the fact that a wrong --settings path also yields a session with no guard and no signal, there is currently no positive confirmation anywhere that marimba is running guarded. Two Questionables came from this and both were decided on 2026-08-21: Q2 makes an unrunnable parser fail closed, and Q4 adds a session-start banner so the condition is visible before the first tool call rather than after it.
  3. Fence 2 over-denies, and that lands on this workstream's own build. A marimba session cannot author the guard's test fixtures with a shell heredoc, because the fixture text contains the denied shapes and its own guard denies the write. The fixtures must be authored with the file-writing tools, which carry no command field. This is written into task 4 so it is not discovered mid-build.
