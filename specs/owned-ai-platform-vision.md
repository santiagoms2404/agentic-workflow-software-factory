## 2. The vision in words

### Proposed north star

> Give people a personal AI system they control, which grows with them, connects their software and physical environments, and helps them create the capabilities they need through an approachable, customizable interface. Preserve private operation and let customers choose their models, services, devices, and permitted uses of their data.

### The full ambition

The product's identity is a personal AI environment with continuity: permitted knowledge, preferences, workflows, applications, interfaces, and operating history can develop over time. A user can discover a capability, adapt it, ask for a new one, observe what happens, correct it, and keep the resulting improvement. Better models can become replaceable sources of intelligence within that environment. A customer's system should not have to start over whenever a model changes.

Q1 and SRC11 establish that the personal AI stays with its user while shared home or workplace installations have separate governance. The personal system can have profiles such as Home, Work, and Away with different behavior, permissions, and tools. Detailed rules for selecting a profile, separating its information, and authorizing access remain to be designed.

The owner describes two connected product offerings. The **AI software product** is the visual place where people interact with, customize, create, and govern their system. The **physical installation product** brings supported compute, devices, sensing, interfaces, and automation into homes, apartments, buildings, and offices. SRC20 accepts independently available software and physical-installation services, with optional supported hardware and bundled offerings. What each package includes, its compatible configurations, and its service terms remain open. An OS-like experience is a product metaphor at this stage, not a decision to build a new operating-system kernel.

The physical ambition includes cameras, microphones, smart glasses, wristbands, rings and other wearable/cosmetic forms, adapters, sensors, screens, access-control devices, local compute and cloud GPUs. Compute capacity such as GPUs and RAM is distinct from electrical power supply, renewable generation, and storage. These are areas to evaluate, not a promise to manufacture or support every device. Shared spaces add occupants, guests, workers, and separate rights that an installation owner cannot silently absorb.

The software connects the owner's ongoing work into a coherent experience. AWSF contributes governed creation and verification. Marimba contributes the personal interaction and orchestration layer. ### E3 — Business hypotheses

The first recommendation is to sell dependable operation: supported hardware, installation, maintenance, optional encrypted backup, remote access, optional cloud compute, and creator services. **Owner-proposed data organization and collection service (SRC12):** Help customers make their data understandable and useful for their chosen purposes. The following scope is recommended for refinement:

- **DS1 — Organize existing information:** Identify eligible sources, preserve their origin, and help customers structure, label, and find information. Review changes before applying them and keep personal, work, and shared information appropriately separated.
- **DS2 — Plan purposeful collection:** Decide what information a desired capability actually needs, how to collect it accurately, whose permission is required, where it stays, who can access it, and how long to retain it. Avoid collecting unrelated information merely because a sensor or connector permits it.
- **DS3 — Check usefulness and quality:** Identify missing, inconsistent, stale, or duplicate information and help customers maintain useful records. Measure whether the data supports the intended task rather than rewarding collection volume.

Organizing information does not grant permission to train on it, submit it to a paid-contribution program, or license it to a buyer. Those remain separate Q6 decisions. Rights concerning other people still apply.

**Business choices still open:** Decide one-time setup versus recurring assistance, what is self-service versus assisted, supported data sources, price, and the exact deliverables. This is a proposed offering, not approval to begin collection or process real records.

The utility analogy is about availability and understandable service levels. Becoming an energy provider is a separate business. Measure wall power, duty cycle, cooling, hardware depreciation, energy prices, and required availability before proposing renewable-energy economics.

A personal Marimba need not mean training a foundation model. Owner-controlled memory, retrieval, workflows, and model selection may provide personalization. Fine-tuning should be compared with an untuned baseline on named tasks, using eligible data and the existing training restrictions.

### E4 — What scalability and self-building mean here

- **G1 — Across purposes:** Health, personal administration, work, creation, and physical automation are applications of the same environment. A domain app should not define the whole platform.
- **G2 — Across a customer's lifetime:** Permitted knowledge, preferences, and capabilities can develop without a forced reset when a model, device, or app changes. Customers can inspect, correct, export, and remove supported information.
- **G3 — Across installations:** Individuals, households, buildings, and offices introduce different users, resources, and authorities. Capacity growth and shared-space privacy require evidence, not just the same screen deployed more widely.
- **G4 — Across intelligence and compute:** Customers can use appropriate local or authorized external models and compute. The system explains capability, cost, and privacy trade-offs rather than claiming one model is best at everything.
- **G5 — Across software lifetimes:** SRC23 adds durable, reusable, and short-lived personal tools. Their lifecycle should scale without creating a company-hosted service or a manual maintenance obligation for every generated instance. E7 describes the confirmed separation of lasting customer information from replaceable tools, with implementation details open.

**Recommended meaning of software that builds itself:** The user requests or approves a change, the system proposes or generates a bounded implementation, checks it in an isolated environment, shows what will change, and activates it under the required authority with a recovery path. Useful corrections may improve memory, configuration, tools, or code. Model training is a separate activity. Generated code must not grant itself permissions or rewrite the controls that authorize it.

**Personal replacements for subscription apps:** Target the customer's actual jobs and compare capabilities, migration, reliability, maintenance, and total cost. External content, network effects, licensed services, and payment infrastructure may remain necessary. Private operation can be valuable even when it does not eliminate every subscription. Comparable or better capability must be demonstrated for a named use, not promised for all apps.

### Risks

- **R1 — Too many businesses at once:** Marketplace, developer platform, hardware, energy, and data exchange have distinct customers and operating requirements. Their coexistence in a vision does not justify a simultaneous launch.
- **R2 — Local does not automatically mean safe:** Isolation, controlled network access, updates, recovery, and a maintenance owner remain necessary. Open source and successful factory tests do not establish marketplace-app safety by themselves.
- **R3 — Data credits could undermine privacy:** Revenue tied to disclosure creates an incentive conflict. Consent does not alone establish lawful processing, low re-identification risk, or a market for the resulting data. Sensitive health data and other people's information require particular care.
- **R4 — Hardware cannot guarantee arbitrary performance:** Performance promises need specific configurations and workload bounds. Hardware support also creates repair, replacement, and ongoing support obligations.

The most dangerous execution failure is building an entire ecosystem before proving repeated usefulness. No fatal contradiction was identified in the broad goal. The closest is promising ownership while making continued usefulness depend on the platform's permission or subscription.

### Customer hypothesis

A plausible first user wants useful private AI without operating containers, selecting inference settings, or debugging updates. They would value an accurate preview, understandable permissions, predictable performance, and an undo path. Setup failures, silent cloud fallback, abandoned apps, and misleading cost claims could drive them away.

SRC6 and SRC8 previously selected a wellness-focused customer/pilot direction. SRC9 corrects the product framing: the customer wants a personal AI environment that can serve many purposes and grow across software and physical contexts. The first pilot audience and the balance between individual and shared installations require review. Everyday customization and creation are part of the product ambition, while current public-user factory restrictions still apply. Interviews, observed use, and willingness to pay must test any chosen starting audience.

### F16 — Open source permits commercial competition

The [Open Source Definition](https://opensource.org/osd) requires rights to obtain source, modify, redistribute, and use software without excluding businesses or fields of use. A restriction against competitors or commercial hosting cannot simply be added while retaining that definition. Publicly visible code alone is insufficient. Code licensing does not grant access to customer records, deployment credentials, or a company's brand.

This makes customer freedom and commercial exclusivity different objectives. Q3 compares the trade-offs rather than assuming one license maximizes both.

### F17 — License obligations have different triggers and boundaries

The Q3 comparison draws on [Apache License 2.0](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0), [MPL 2.0](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/MPL/2.0/), its [official FAQ](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/MPL/2.0/FAQ/), and [AGPLv3's text hosted by SPDX](https://spdx.org/licenses/AGPL-3.0-only.html). Relevant provisions include Apache sections 2–6, MPL sections 1.10 and 3 with FAQ questions 11/17, and AGPL sections 2/5/6/13. Direct retrieval of GNU's HTML page was unavailable, so the consulted AGPL text is the SPDX copy.

AGPL section 6 also has installation-information obligations for certain consumer-device distributions. Hardware plans would need specific review. AGPL section 2 distinguishes use of the program from outputs containing covered code. A personal software factory therefore needs an explicit review of its templates and generated dependencies, not an assumption that every output inherits its license or that none can.

### Q3 — Open-source and external-service policy

**Question:** Which layers must be open source, and how should local models, external services, and customers' existing AI subscriptions fit?

**Owner priority (SRC9):** Accommodate supported personal AI subscriptions and provider/model choice as part of the system. This is a product priority, not a claim that every provider permits the same integrations.

**Status after SRC14:** Record the current recommendation as provisional and return to it when evidence and release readiness justify a decision. No license has been adopted. The earlier blanket recommendation to make platform/app code open needs a defined scope, business model, and review of existing rights. The following is an engineering/product comparison based on F16–F19. A qualified open-source licensing lawyer should review the actual code relationships, contribution terms, hardware distribution, and release policy before adoption.

#### What the choice controls

A software license governs rights to the code. Publishing code does not publish customer data, give outsiders control of installed systems, or supply free models, cloud compute, support, or warranties. A source release also needs usable build/install information to make practical independence possible.

Commercial reuse is part of genuine open source. License conditions may require notices or sharing covered source in specified circumstances. They do not force competitors to pay us, contribute patches to our repository, or stop competing. Brand protection and contracts for actual services are separate tools. Already-granted rights to compliant users of released versions cannot be treated as a subscription that the company can simply withdraw.

#### License routes

“Copyleft” means that specified covered code must remain available under the required open terms when the license's conditions are triggered. It does not mean every file, data record, or program near that code becomes public.

| ID | Route | Main benefit | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LC1 | Permissive open source, represented by Apache-2.0 | Broad reuse with notice obligations and an explicit contributor patent grant. Downstream proprietary derivatives are permitted. | A competitor can build a closed product using the code. No requirement to return its improvements. |
| LC2 | File-level copyleft, MPL-2.0 | Distributed modifications to covered files stay under MPL. Separately authored files can use other licenses. | Hosted-only server changes need not be shared. It does not prevent competing products or proprietary additions. Browser-delivered code can count as distribution. |
| LC3 | Strong network copyleft, AGPL-3.0 | Adds a source-offer obligation to users interacting remotely with a modified version, alongside distribution obligations. | Broader covered-work boundaries need review. It allows commercial hosting and does not automatically require a fee or publication of unrelated software. |
| LC4 | Source-available terms, such as FSL | Can restrict competing uses during a defined period while making source inspectable. | Restricted versions are not open source. Conditions can limit independent reuse and require separate commercial agreements. |
| LC5 | Proprietary software | Reserves more reuse and distribution rights to the vendor. | Customer modifications, third-party maintenance, and company-closure independence depend on additional rights and practical provisions. Source secrecy alone does not provide reliability or security. |

LC1–LC3 are license families/routes, not business plans. A copyleft source offer goes to the entitled recipients or users. It is not a compulsory contribution to our main project. A customer building an app with our factory does not automatically license that app under the factory's license. Copied templates, dependencies, and combined works still need review.

#### Business models are a separate choice

| ID | Model | Fit and trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BM1 | Open product plus paid hardware and services | Best current fit for Q2. Charge for installation, data assistance, support, maintenance, hosting, backup, and dependable operation. Revenue must cover service costs. Others may offer competing services. |
| BM2 | Open core plus proprietary add-ons | Can sell additional software features. The boundary must leave the promised local system complete and usable. Paywalling essential export, permissions, recovery, or ongoing local activation would undermine Q2. |
| BM3 | Open-source and alternative commercial licenses for the same code | Can sell different terms to buyers who need them. Requires sufficient rights over all covered contributions and dependencies. It cannot turn a compliant open-source user into a mandatory paying customer. |

Using different open licenses for the core and creator tools is distinct from BM2's closed add-ons. A contribution-provenance process such as a DCO does not automatically supply BM3's relicensing rights. Decide contribution terms transparently before depending on that business option. No contributor agreement or new project license is selected here.

#### Risks to take seriously

- **LR1 — Competition and uncertain revenue:** Source access lowers the cost of reuse, while service delivery still consumes money and staff time. Larger competitors could offer a convenient competing product. Neither a community nor a service margin follows automatically from opening code.
- **LR2 — Integration and compliance burden:** License compatibility, creator-app boundaries, browser code, generated templates, hardware distribution, and contributor ownership require review. More restrictive sharing requirements can make some integrations less attractive, while lighter requirements leave more competitor changes private. This adoption trade-off is a hypothesis to test with intended creators and integrators.
- **LR3 — Maintenance and trust:** Public development needs issue triage, security reporting, release discipline, documentation, and clear support responsibilities. Source visibility can help review, but does not establish safety. Forks may diverge and create customer confusion. Protect official branding without promising that others cannot build alternatives.
- **LR4 — Overclaiming ownership or changing course:** Open code alone does not make cloud dependencies portable, hardware repairable, or model weights freely licensed. A later restrictive strategy cannot simply erase earlier grants and may damage community trust. Existing third-party code cannot be relicensed merely because we combine it into the product.

#### Provisional recommendation recorded for later review (SRC14)

- **LP1 — A complete open local system:** Keep the software needed for personal operation, profiles, permissions, export/restore, the local interface, and supported local execution independently usable. Shortlist MPL-2.0 as the core candidate because this vision emphasizes customer customization, app creation, and varied integrations. This is a provisional balance, not a claim that it maximizes every objective.
- **LP2 — Low-friction creation and integration:** Prefer Apache-2.0 for our own independently licensable SDKs, connector scaffolding, and reusable templates where appropriate. Design explicit component boundaries, then review actual code/dependency relationships. A separate process or API is not an automatic legal exemption. Preserve existing third-party licenses.
- **LP3 — Clear app, model, and data boundaries:** Keep first-party apps and the initial curated catalog open-source in code, with compatible per-app licenses rather than one forced license for everything. Private personal creations need not be submitted to the catalog. Model weights, datasets, hardware designs, trademarks, and online services need their own terms. Support a useful local baseline and explicitly chosen external providers.
- **LP4 — Earn revenue through continuing value:** Prefer BM1. Keep customers' existing local capabilities useful without renewal. Sell supported hardware, integration, data organization/collection guidance, and optional operating services. Treat dual licensing or closed add-ons as separate future choices rather than assumed revenue. Protect brand identity and official service quality without claiming exclusive rights over open code.

**Why this is the current recommendation:** It prioritizes the owner's customer-control promise and ability to create a unique system. The proposed balance asks for sharing distributed core-file improvements while leaving room for independently authored applications. Its deliberate cost is allowing competing hosted implementations to retain server-only changes. If that cost is unacceptable, LC3 is the stronger open-source candidate. If preventing competing hosting itself is essential, LC4 merits review and the product must accurately describe the restricted components.

**Subscription boundary retained:** Verify each provider's permitted integration, authentication, quotas, model access, and charging arrangement. Consumer subscriptions and developer APIs are not interchangeable. Keep credentials out of model-visible context and disclose where processing occurs. An external feature cannot be described as local.

**Before selecting terms:** Inventory ownership, contribution permissions, existing licenses, distribution/deployment modes, and generated-code paths. Test the proposed policy against a private personal app, an independent marketplace app, a shipped home appliance, and a competing hosted service. Obtain specialist review and an explicit owner decision before publishing or changing licenses. This document does not relicense AWSF, Marimba, Smart Health, BrainOS/Evo, or any dependency, and grants no runtime authority.

**Deferred owner choice:** Is reciprocity for distributed core modifications sufficient, or must modified hosted versions also offer their source to users? Keep the former as the working recommendation for initial customization/integration flexibility. Neither choice prevents lawful commercial competition.

**Review triggers:** Return to Q3 when material evidence becomes available, and before publishing under a license or committing to contributor/commercial terms that depend on the choice:

- **RV1 — Technical and rights evidence:** Concrete component boundaries, generated-code behavior, dependency licenses, and contribution ownership permit a specific legal compatibility review.
- **RV2 — Adoption evidence:** Customer, creator, and integrator feedback shows whether the proposed rights and obligations help or hinder the intended uses.
- **RV3 — Business evidence:** Service costs, willingness to pay, distribution plans, and competing hosted offerings clarify whether BM1 and the proposed reciprocity boundary are sustainable.

No calendar date, execution task, or automatic license change is created. A later review must record an explicit owner decision before release terms change.

## 11. Turning the founder's vision into product decisions

These are proposed working habits informed by F12 and this product's customer-control goal. They are not claims about Jobs's entire personality or a formula for reproducing Apple's success.

- **J1 — Make the promise concrete:** Review one sentence describing what the customer can accomplish and one demonstration showing it. Keep the wider vision, but select a small number of outcomes for each release.
- **J2 — Exercise judgment through prototypes:** Walk through real onboarding, a useful result, a mistake, and recovery. Replace vague demands for “better UX” with specific observed friction and a revised prototype.
- **J3 — Integrate the experience:** Inspect transitions among discovery, permissions, Marimba, execution, payment, and support. Customers should experience one coherent product while retaining choices and an exit path.
- **J4 — Combine conviction with correction:** Defend the customer-control purpose. Change features when evidence contradicts assumptions. Reward people who expose problems and challenge decisions respectfully. High standards do not require humiliation or unquestioned founder authority.
- **J5 — Protect focus:** Maintain explicit exclusions in each plan. Ask what customer problem would remain unsolved if a proposed feature were removed. Admit work through story and acceptance evidence rather than resemblance to a famous company's roadmap.

**Review questions:** Does the demonstration express the vision? Can a customer understand and control it? What evidence would make us change the design? Record decisions through Q/US/AC references so taste becomes an inspectable product contract.
