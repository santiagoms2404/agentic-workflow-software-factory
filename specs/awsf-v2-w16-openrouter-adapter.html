In Plain Language

This section explains the whole plan without technical vocabulary. Everything after it says the same things precisely, for the people — and machines — building it.

What this is about

Your factory hires its workers from exactly two agencies, and it pays both the same way: a flat monthly fee that buys a certain amount of work and then stops until the month turns over. When a month runs dry, the factory waits. There is no third agency it knows how to call.

There is a second thing, and it matters more than it sounds. Neither agency ever tells the factory what a job actually cost. One cannot say at all, because a share of a monthly fee is not a price. The other quotes a figure, but the figure is the factory doing its own sums from a price list it keeps in a drawer — which is a guess that looks like a receipt. A while ago somebody noticed that and made the factory label it honestly: it now shows those numbers with a little "about" in front of them.

Why now

Because a third agency turned out to already be reachable, and somebody checked rather than assumed. The tool the factory already uses to talk to one of its two agencies can also talk to a marketplace — a middleman that resells access to hundreds of different AI workers, including ones anybody can download and run, and bills by the job on a card instead of by the month.

A single test job was sent through it. It cost two thousandths of a cent. Everything the marketplace said back was in a language the factory already understands — every single message type, with no exceptions. And crucially, the bill it sent back looks like a real bill rather than a guess. If that holds up, it would be the first job in the factory's history whose cost is a fact instead of an estimate.

So this is cheap work sitting on top of a measurement that has already been taken. That is the reason to do it now rather than later.

What we are building

Think of it as a second phone line to the same switchboard. The factory already has one line into that switchboard, and that line is locked to one agency on purpose — the lock is there so that nobody can quietly redirect a line that has been inspected and approved to somewhere that has not been. We are not picking that lock. We are running a second line, with its own inspection.

Four things have to happen, in order.

  • Teach the factory how the new agency spells a worker's name. Today it refuses any name with a slash in it, and every name at this marketplace has one. That refusal is not a bug — on the old line, a slash would smuggle in a second agency's name. On the new line it is just how names are written. So the new line gets its own spelling rule, and the old line keeps its rule exactly as it was. There is a second, quieter place in the factory that also refuses these names, which nobody had noticed; it gets fixed too.
  • Build the second line, and stop losing count when a worker stumbles. Sometimes the marketplace is busy and the tool quietly asks again — two or three times — inside what the factory counts as a single conversation. Right now the factory writes those moments down as "something happened here that I do not recognise" and shows you the first fragment of the line. After this, it writes down what actually happened: attempt two of three, waited four seconds, then succeeded. It still counts as one job, and there is a good reason: the factory's whole budget system counts the number of times it picks up the phone, not the number of times somebody says hello. Changing that would mean tampering with the one piece of bookkeeping that stops two jobs starting at once and both slipping under the limit.
  • Earn the right to say what a job cost — rather than just claiming it. This is the part with a trap in it, and the factory has already fallen into it once. The other agency's numbers looked exactly this convincing until somebody from a different background read the tool's own workings and found it was doing the arithmetic itself. So four things have to be true before the factory is allowed to call this a real price, and one of them has not been checked yet: read the tool's own workings for this new line and confirm it is passing the marketplace's bill through rather than calculating its own. If that turns out to be false, the honest answer is to keep the "about" label, write down why, and lose nothing else. There is also a small embarrassment to fix first: the factory rounds money to the nearest cent, so two thousandths of a cent currently displays as zero — which would turn a real price into something indistinguishable from free.
  • Wire it up, switched off, and be honest about one thing it breaks. The new line gets registered but arrives with the switch in the off position; turning it on is your decision, taken separately. And there is a collision: when the factory has a worker build something, it insists the review be done by a different agency, and it works out which one by elimination from a pair. A pair is not three. Put all three agencies on one job today and the factory refuses the whole thing before it starts — correctly, but with a message that says "I found three" without saying which three. This plan makes the refusal say who. It does not change the rule, because changing it is your call, not the plan's.

Three things you decided on 2026-09-15

Each was written up with its options and the case for each, with no answer pre-selected. You answered all three. Two of your answers went against what was recommended, and the recommendation is kept next to your decision rather than quietly rewritten to agree — otherwise nobody can see later what the trade-off actually was.

  • When the tool quietly tries again, has the factory used up one of its allowance or three? — you said one. That was also the recommendation, and not as a preference: the allowance counts how many times the factory picks up the phone, and a retry is the same call. Nothing about the plan changes. The factory still has to write down that the retry happened, because those failed tries cost real money and real time.
  • Should the cheap new workers be allowed to do the reviewing, or only the building? — you said reviewing is fine. The recommendation was building only, and the reason is worth keeping in view rather than arguing about again: the reviewer is the single role whose work nothing downstream checks. Here is the good news about your answer: it costs nothing to carry out. The factory never asked a reviewer to be strong — only to be a different supplier from the one that built the thing — so nothing had to be unlocked. There is also a real safety net already in place: a reviewer has to fill in a structured form, and the form is checked, so a reviewer that answers badly is more likely to fail the check than to slip a hollow review through.
  • Does the marketplace count as one supplier, when it can supply workers from everywhere? — you said yes, one supplier, and no limit on how many marketplace workers a job may use. The recommendation agreed on the first half and asked for a limit of one per job; you took the first half alone. That turns out to be safe, and it was checked rather than assumed. The thing the limit was meant to prevent — a marketplace worker reviewing another marketplace worker — is already impossible: the factory would see one supplier on both sides, be unable to work out "the other one", and refuse the job. The one real consequence is that more jobs can now end up naming three suppliers at once, which the factory also refuses. So the part of the plan that makes that refusal say who matters more than it did yesterday.

How we will know it worked

  • A job runs on a worker from the new marketplace, with the same rules, the same sealed workspace and the same stopping points as any other job.
  • When the tool stumbles and tries again, you can read what happened in plain words, and the job still counts as one.
  • Either the factory shows a real price, with all four pieces of proof sitting in the repository where anyone can check them, or it keeps the honest "about" label and the missing piece is written down. Those are the only two acceptable endings; quietly claiming the first without the proof is the failure this plan is shaped to prevent.
  • The existing line to the other agency is byte for byte what it was, and a test says so rather than a person remembering.
  • Nothing about how the factory handles passwords or keys changed. The key for the new line lives where that tool already keeps its own keys, and the factory never looks at it.
  • The new line ships switched off.

What this deliberately is not

It is not a cheaper way to run everything — routing decisions stay yours. It is not a change to how the factory decides who reviews whom. It is not a new way to watch jobs or to send work outside this machine. And it is not related to the other unfinished piece of work about a third company's assistant, which is stuck waiting for evidence about whether it can be safely contained. This one has no such gap, which is exactly why it is worth doing now.

The one-paragraph version
Your factory can only hire from two agencies, pays both a flat monthly fee, and has never once been told what a job actually cost. A third source is already reachable through a tool it uses every day, a test job through it cost two thousandths of a cent, and everything that came back was in a language the factory already speaks. This plan builds a second, separately inspected line to that source — leaving the first line untouched — teaches the factory how these workers' names are spelled, makes it say plainly when the tool quietly retried instead of writing down a shrug, and fixes the rounding that would show a real price as zero. Then it does the part that matters most: it refuses to let the factory claim it knows what a job cost until four specific pieces of proof exist, one of which nobody has checked yet. If that proof does not turn up, the honest label stays and nothing else is lost. The line arrives switched off. Three real decisions were put to you with the cases laid out and no answer pre-selected, and on 2026-09-15 you took all three: a quiet retry costs one turn, the cheap new workers may review as well as build, and the marketplace counts as a single supplier with no limit on how many of its workers a job may use. Two of those went against the recommendation, which is kept beside them so the trade-off stays readable. Neither needs a line of code — the factory already only asked a reviewer to be a different supplier, and it already refuses a job whose two sides come from the same one. What your answers do change is that one awkward truth matters more, not less: with three suppliers on a single job, the factory's rule for picking a reviewer stops working, because it picks by elimination from a pair — so the refusal had better say which three.

Purpose

AWSF has two verified worker routes and both are subscriptions: Claude Pro through the Claude CLI, and ChatGPT Plus through pi on its openai-codex provider. Both are metered by a monthly window the factory cannot top up, neither can report what a run actually cost, and neither can reach a model outside its vendor's own family. This workstream adds a third route that is none of those things — key-billed rather than subscription-metered, open-weight as well as frontier, and priced by the provider rather than estimated by the client.

It is a second adapter, not a widening of the first. PI_PROVIDER is pinned in core/src/adapters/pi-codex.ts with a rationale that is still correct: a different provider through the same binary is a different protocol, a different auth path and a different set of captured bytes, and accepting one from config would let an unreviewed route inherit a reviewed adapter's descriptor tests. Nothing in this plan edits that constant, that comment, or that adapter's argv.

What it inherits for free, and this is the whole reason it is cheap: pi's tool boundary is already verified on this machine and its stream vocabulary is already decoded. A single-turn probe on 2026-09-15 emitted twenty-one events and every one of them is a branch pi-codex-stream.ts already handles. The adapter is therefore a descriptor and a registration, not a parser.

Problem

Four things stand between the measured probe and a route a phase can run on. Two were named in the request and are real. One was named with a symptom that does not exist yet, and correcting that changes the work rather than cancelling it. One was not named at all and is the hardest.

1 — The selector guard refuses every real OpenRouter id, in two places

SAFE_MODEL_SELECTOR at core/src/adapters/pi-codex.ts:118 is /^[A-Za-z0-9][A-Za-z0-9._-]{0,127}$/. It refuses deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash-0731, z-ai/glm-5.2 and ~deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash-latest alike. Both of its narrowings are deliberate and documented in the comment above it, and both reasons survive onto this route unchanged: a / would name a second provider from inside a flag that has already been told which provider to use, and a : would set --thinking from the model field and silently outrank the level the agent's config put on the line.

The slash reason is the one that changes, and only on this route. On OpenRouter a / is not a second provider — it is how OpenRouter spells a model, and --provider openrouter is still the only provider on the line. The colon reason does not change at all and the refusal must stay.

There is a second refusal site the request did not name. resolveSelector in core/src/adapters/catalog.ts:31 carries the same regex, plus a rule that a selector must contain exactly one colon. It is a declared surface rather than a live gate: grepping core/src, core/test and dashboard/src finds no caller outside core/test/unit/adapters/catalog-registry.test.ts. A plan that widened the adapter and left the catalog alone would ship a route the catalog says is unrepresentable, which is exactly the kind of quiet disagreement invariant 12 exists to stop one level up.

2 — pi retries inside a single phase, and the host cannot see what it means

A free-tier run produced three events the decoder has no branch for: auto_retry_start carrying {attempt, maxAttempts, delayMs, errorMessage} and auto_retry_end carrying {success, attempt, finalError}, from an upstream 429.

Unhandled does not mean silent, and the difference is the whole of this task. The decoder's default: branch emits sequencer.notice("unknown-provider-event", …) — so three retries already produce three notices today, carrying the line's first 200 bytes. What is missing is not visibility but meaning: nothing says how many attempts there were, what the delay was, whether the last one succeeded, or that the run is still one call. The honest statement of the gap is that a retry arrives as an unrecognized line rather than as a retry.

3 — The surface the symptom was stated against does not exist

Correction to the request, recorded rather than quietly fixed

The request describes a run-failures surface showing "one failure where three happened". There is no such surface in this tree. A sweep for run-failures, runFailures, RunFailure and failuresFor across core/src, dashboard/ and specs/ returns two unrelated prose matches in v1 build prompts and nothing else. What exists is the event log, whose summarizeNotice (dashboard/src/event-summary.ts:58) renders any notice generically as code: message, the journal, and core/src/observability/run-report.ts.

This does not cancel the work; it relocates it. The projection and the event log are where a retry must become legible, and because summarizeNotice is generic over the code, a new notice code renders with no dashboard change at all. The plan builds against the surfaces that exist, and it does not invent a failure surface to justify a decision it can take on the event log.

This is the spine's own R1 discipline pointed back at a request: a symptom stated against a surface nobody has built is an overstatement of the same family as calling a camera a wall, and correcting it is cheaper here than after the code is shaped around it.

4 — Inversion admits exactly two providers, and this makes three

This was not in the request and it is the hardest thing in the workstream. providerPairFrom (core/src/workflow/review-routing.ts:36) takes the providers of a compiled recipe's agent phases, de-duplicates them, and throws InvalidReviewInversion unless exactly two remain. Both call sites pass a whole recipe's phases: production-run.ts:1158 maps every kind === "agent" phase, and review-phase.ts:497 builds the same list from the recipe. A tier-2 recipe with a Claude planner, an OpenRouter builder and a Claude reviewer has two providers and works. The same recipe with a Claude planner, a Codex builder and an OpenRouter documenter has three, and is refused before any child starts — at preflight, at zero cost, with a message that names a count rather than a culprit.

Solution

A second adapter file, a route-owned selector guard, one new notice code, one rendering fix, and a registration that ships disabled. Nothing else changes, and three things are explicitly left alone: PI_PROVIDER, ENV_ALLOWLIST, and the argv of the Codex route.

DECIDED Q1 — Does a pi-level retry count against the call ceiling? (task 5)

Decided (2026-09-15): one call — the ceiling counts host-launched processes. Owner verdict via the exported plan-sota-review v1 block. This matches the recommendation below, so task 5 is unchanged: it adds the notice and touches no part of the ledger.

Recommendation, as written before the verdict: one call. Not on preference — on the ledger's existing shape, which already answers this and would have to be redesigned to answer differently.

What the ledger already is. core/src/execution/call-budget.ts reserves before launch and spends on GO. reserve() is synchronous by contract, and a unit test pins that it is not an AsyncFunction, because the check and the commit must happen in one turn of the event loop or two concurrent launches can both slip under the ceiling. A call is therefore a host-launched process, and a retry inside one process has no launch, no registration and no reservation to attach to.

What "many" would have cost. Charging each attempt means mutating the ledger from a stream event, mid-run, after the reservation has been made — which is exactly the await-between-check-and-commit hole reserve()'s synchronicity exists to close. It also produces a ceiling that can be exceeded by a provider's own backoff policy, which the owner never authorised and cannot see in advance. A T0 task with a ceiling of one would fail on a transient 429 rather than on anything about the work.

What "one call" does not excuse, and it is now a constraint on task 5. It is not a licence to leave retries invisible: tokens spent on a failed attempt are real and the wall-clock delay is real. The provider-retry notice is what pays that debt, and it is why task 5 exists at all. The third option — a separate retry budget — stays on the table as a named future upgrade if a run is ever observed retrying enough to matter. It needs a number nobody has measured, so proposing one now would be a guess wearing a threshold's clothes.

DECIDED Q2 — Are open-weight routes admissible for reviewer phases, given routing.review: invert-provider? (task 11)

Decided (2026-09-15): yes — any distinct provider may review. Owner verdict via the exported plan-sota-review v1 block. The owner chose against the recommendation below, which argued for worker phases only.

What this decision costs to implement: nothing

It needs no code change, and that is a finding rather than a convenience. Nothing in core/src/workflow/review-routing.ts asks a reviewer to be strong; oppositeProvider asks only that it be different, and neither call site carries an allowlist of permitted reviewer providers. So an open-weight reviewer was already mechanically admissible, and this verdict settles a policy rather than removing a fence. Task 11 therefore records the decision and writes no code for it — and any session that finds itself adding a reviewer-provider allowlist to implement this has misread the verdict backwards.

Recommendation, as written before the verdict: worker phases only, for now — and revisited on measured review quality rather than on principle. Not taken.

What the mechanism actually asks of a reviewer. Inversion exists so that the code is judged by a model family that did not write it. Nothing in review-routing.ts asks a reviewer to be strong; it asks it to be different. So an open-weight route is mechanically admissible today, and this question was about judgement rather than about the code.

The argument for "worker only", retained because it names the residual this decision now carries. The reviewer is the one role whose output the factory cannot check with a gate — a builder's claim is tested by the suite, a reviewer's claim is tested by nothing. This repository has already recorded that the defects which mattered were found by driving and by looking at a screen rather than by a green suite, which is the reviewer's job description. Putting the cheapest model on the one role with no downstream check was argued to be the wrong place to spend a saving first.

First, the comparison the recommendation asked for is still the thing that would settle quality empirically: run the same candidates past an open-weight reviewer and a subscription reviewer and compare what each found against what the gates and the owner found. That is now an evaluation this decision makes possible rather than a gate in front of it. Second, the reviewer envelope is typed and its completeness is already gate-checked, so a reviewer that answers badly is more likely to fail the envelope than to pass a hollow review silently — which is the mitigation that already exists, and it is a real one. The third option — a per-recipe opt-in — is no longer needed: with any distinct provider admissible, a recipe declaring an open-weight reviewer is an ordinary recipe.

DECIDED Q3 — Does OpenRouter count as a distinct provider for inversion, when it can serve both sides of a review? (task 11)

What the dropped cap does and does not change — checked against the code, not assumed

The case the cap was meant to prevent is already closed by a rule that exists. A recipe routing the builder to openrouter:z-ai/glm-5.2 and the reviewer to openrouter:deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash-0731 resolves to one distinct provider, and providerPairFrom refuses anything but exactly two — inversion is undefined over a set of one. What the dropped cap does change is exposure to the three-provider collision, and it raises the value of task 11. With openrouter now admissible on the reviewer side as well as the builder side (Q2), more recipes can reach three distinct providers across their agent phases — and every one of those is refused at preflight. Why the provider string is the right unit, and this half of the recommendation was taken. The model identity this route reports is stream-authoritative — message_end carries provider, model and api — which is stronger than the Codex route's route-attributed identity. But "which vendor really served this" is a claim about OpenRouter's own routing, which AWSF cannot observe and OpenRouter is free to change between calls. Making inversion depend on the upstream vendor would make a boundary depend on a fact the host reads from a field it cannot verify, which is the camera-called-a-wall pattern this spine exists to catch. The provider string is what the host launched and can prove.

The case the cap made, retained so the alternative stays readable. Capping a recipe at one OpenRouter phase would have kept the pair honest without a new mechanism, making inversion between openrouter and a subscription route the only shape available, so a review always crossed a real auth and billing boundary rather than two selections from one pool. If the owner ever takes the second option — the upstream vendor as the unit — the work is larger than task 11 and belongs to its own workstream: it needs a vendor-identity field the contract does not have and a source of truth for it that is not the provider's own self-report.

Notes

Why this is not an extension of W09

W09 is the agy adapter and it is gated on an evidence gap this workstream does not share. That route is write-capable, its containment is detection-bounded rather than prevention-bounded — path-policy compares a worktree fingerprint after the provider returns, and sandbox-broker degrades to tool-policy where bwrap is absent, which is the case on this machine — and it waits on two things that do not exist: a capture settling whether a tool event arrives before or after the provider executed that tool, and a containment capture across the WSL/Windows boundary.

None of that applies here. This route rides pi's tool boundary, which is expressed as --tools and --no-tools flags the adapter already builds and the Codex suite already asserts, on a CLI whose permission surface has been exercised. Its parser is not a gap at all — it is a 691-line file with fixtures, and the probe found nothing it does not handle. The two are separate workstreams because they are separate risks, and folding this into W09 would make a route with no evidence gap wait on one that has two.

Two prompt surfaces, and why this plan has both

The plan-sota ticket workflow says the ticket directory is the only prompt artifact and that no <stem>-build-prompts.md should be authored. This repository's own fence disagrees, mechanically. resolvePlanSources pairs every .html in specs/ with <stem>-build-prompts.md, and where a ticket set exists ticket-plan-sync.test.ts asserts the prompts file exists and that each ticket's ## Build prompt body is byte-identical to its ### Tnn — title block in Section B.

A fence that fails the suite beats a convention in a skill file, so this plan ships both, and the ## Handoff sections stay empty at authoring time and are appended to by predecessor sessions. The residual is real and is named rather than hidden: an appended Handoff does not break byte-identity, because the fence compares only the ## Build prompt body, but a session that edits a build prompt must edit both files in the same commit. That is written into this plan's ticket README.md so nobody re-derives it from a red test.
