In Plain Language

This section explains the whole plan without technical vocabulary. Everything after it says the same things precisely, for the people — and machines — building it.

Asking several minds the same question

The owner already does something valuable by hand. When a question is hard, he asks three to five different artificial-intelligence assistants the same question at once, reads all their answers, and then has one of them merge every answer into a single result. He does this with a tool he wrote himself. It works, and he uses it often enough that the factory — the system that manages the owner's software work — was supposed to learn how to do it too.

The obstacle is a bookkeeping one, and it is more serious than it sounds. The factory keeps a strict record of every assistant it hires: who was hired, what it was allowed to touch, what it said, and what it cost. It does this by being the only thing in the building allowed to open a door and let a worker in. The owner's hand tool opens its own doors. So if the factory hired the tool, the factory would have a record of hiring one worker, while five walked in behind it. Five workers did the job, five bills came due, and the factory's books would show one of everything. That is the actual problem, and it is not a problem with the arithmetic — the books can already record a partial payment perfectly well. It is that nobody wrote down who the other four were.

Why now

Two things changed after the problem was first written down, and they point in opposite directions.

The first is good news. The factory recently learned how to send different questions to different assistants within a single job, and to write down exactly which one answered each part. That capability is what makes the whole idea possible in a new way — before it, "ask a second assistant for an opinion" was not a sentence the factory could even say.

The second is a correction. That new capability is finished but not yet folded into the main line of work; it sits on a side branch, and merging it is a known, sizeable job that another piece of work is already waiting on. And the factory can currently only reach two assistants, both from the same two companies it already pays a monthly fee to. Asking two assistants and then having one of them merge the two answers is barely different from what the factory already does every day. The version worth building needs cheap access to many more assistants, and a separate piece of work is being planned right now to open exactly that door.

What we are building

Nothing new, structurally — and that is the entire point of the plan.

The factory already knows how to run a job as a sequence of numbered steps, where each step is one worker doing one thing and handing its result to the next. Every step already gets a signed entry in the ledger, a permission slip saying what it may touch, a recorded answer, and a line on the bill. So instead of building a new machine that hires five workers at once, this plan says: make each of the five workers its own step. Ask assistant one. Ask assistant two. Ask assistant three. Then a step the factory itself performs, costing nothing, gathers the three answers into one folder. Then a final worker reads the folder and produces the merged result.

Every guarantee the ambitious version was going to be built to provide arrives for free, because a step already has all of them. And one worry answers itself: the steps happen one after another, never at the same time, so two workers can never be editing the same files at once. The owner's own newer hand tool had to solve that problem with a lock. The factory does not need a lock, because it never runs two workers simultaneously in the first place.

The cost of this approach is honest and it is named throughout. The factory's steps are written down in advance, by hand, one list per kind of job. A consultation with five assistants needs a list of five steps, and nothing in the factory yet builds a step list on the spot. That is real work. It is also the exact same real work that another current piece of planning needs, so the two should build it once and share it.

How we know it worked

Three things, in order, and the first two cost nothing.

First: a test, run on this machine with no assistant hired at all, showing that a five-step consultation can be written down, checked against the spending limit, and refused politely if it does not fit — all before a single worker is let in the door. Second: a test proving that a three-assistant consultation produces three separate ledger entries before the first worker is allowed to begin. That is the exact promise the original idea could not keep, and it is checkable for free.

Only after those two pass does anyone spend money. The third is one real consultation, deliberately small — two assistants, the two the factory can already reach — with the bill for each one recorded separately.

What is deliberately not part of this: the owner's hand tool is not being connected to the factory, not in either of its versions, and this plan explains at length why that turned out to be impossible rather than merely unwise. The owner keeps using his tool by hand, exactly as he does today. And this whole piece of work is optional — if the first free test says the idea does not hold up, the honest ending is to write down what was learned and stop, and that counts as finishing.

Purpose

The owner drives multi-model fusion by hand, every week, through a tool he wrote. The v2 spine committed to bringing that capability inside AWSF, preferring a native mode where AWSF spawns the constituent models through its own broker. This workstream decides how — and its first duty is to correct the question, because the fork the spine inherited has three arms rather than two, and the third one costs the least.

The decisive finding is unchanged and it is still the centre of this plan. Only the outer process crosses core/src/execution/transport-broker.ts. No constituent provider launch gets a registration, a reservation id, or its own GO. Everything the source record promised about per-role supervision rests on processes AWSF never sees.

The overstatement stays corrected, and the correction matters. Partial settlement is not blocked by the ledger. settle(id, spent) exists at call-budget.ts:419 and its own comment describes exactly the halfway composite — two workers reach GO, the fuser's registration fails, so two are spent and one goes back. spendOnGo at :400 and releaseOnRegistrationFailure at :409 are thin convenience wrappers over it. The gap is supervision and attribution, not arithmetic. Stating it the other way suggests a ledger redesign that is not needed, and the workstream would be spent on the wrong thing.

What this plan adds to that finding is a second one, of the same kind, which the source record could not have had because it reasoned about the tool from the spine's summary rather than from the tool: the external tool has no non-interactive entry point in either generation. That single fact removes the opaque option from the table, and it is why the fork below closes with two refusals and one choice rather than with a preference.

Problem

AWSF's supervision guarantees are all properties of crossing the broker. transport-broker.ts is the only module in the repository that imports node:child_process (AGENTS.md invariant 3, enforced by the child-process fence meta-test), and its startProcess accepts exactly four registration shapes — task-edge, agent-phase, phase-correction and turn-reconnect. Each one names a reservation that is already held, and the launcher barrier converts that reservation to spend immediately before it releases the child with GO.

A composite adapter crosses that boundary exactly once, for the outer process. The models it consults do not cross it at all. They are children of a process AWSF launched, which means AWSF has no registration for them, no reservation id, no GO, no permission session, no retained envelope, no route provenance and no cost row. It has one of each of those things for the wrapper.

Three things that are already implemented, and must not be rebuilt

  • Reserve the full cost before any launch. reserveComposite(workerCount) at call-budget.ts:392 books compositeCost(workerCount) = workerCount + 1 as one reservation, refusing at the ceiling before anything is mutated. core/test/unit/ceilings.test.ts:223-226 pins the arithmetic.
  • Partial settlement. settle(id, spent) at call-budget.ts:419 is the primitive both convenience wrappers are made of, and its own comment describes the halfway composite.
  • PATH resolution with a blocked result when the executable is absent. resolveExecutable in the broker raises ExecutableNotFound carrying the searched path list, before any child exists.

And the finding that closes the opaque option

Neither generation of the tool has a non-interactive entry point. This was read off disk in both repositories, not inferred from the spine.

  • v1 — the launcher under fusion-harness's executable directory is an eleven-line bash wrapper whose last statement execs node mf.mjs. mf.mjs's own usage text reads mf [LEFT [RIGHT]] [--] [pi options]: it resolves two model selectors against a catalogue, encodes them into an MF_LAUNCH_CONFIG environment entry, and spawnSyncs an interactive session with the extension loaded. Its only non-interactive subcommand is mf models --json, which prints a model catalogue and fuses nothing. There is no mf fuse "<prompt>" that runs one fusion and exits.
  • v2 — there is no executable at all and no launcher directory. The whole tool is a Pi extension loaded with pi -e extensions/fusion-harness/fusion-harness.ts, and every capability is a slash command the owner types into a running TUI: /fh-opinion, /fh-fusion, /fh-debate, /fh-collaborate, /fh-only, /fh-auto-validate. Results land in TUI panels and in a temporary artifact directory.

AWSF's broker takes an executable name and an argv array, resolves the name against PATH, spawns it with a filtered environment and no shell, performs a file-descriptor identity handshake, and requires the adapter to decode a terminal event off the child's stream. An interactive TUI session satisfies none of that. Making the opaque option real therefore begins with building a headless entry point inside the fusion-harness repository — a second repository, and the spine's Shared Invariants name upstream contributions to another repository as owner-side work that cannot land from a managed worktree and never becomes a ticket in any deep plan.

The fork, decided

The spine states the fork with two arms. This plan states it with three, evaluates all three against the code, and closes it. (c) is chosen. The two refusals below are refusals on read evidence; neither rests on preference, and neither rests on (c) having been named last.

(b) Opaque — refused, and here is the promise being given up

The shrunken promise, written out rather than implied. Had (b) been taken, AWSF would have promised exactly this and nothing more: one composite reservation of workers + 1 calls is held before anything launches; one registration exists, for the wrapper; one GO converts the whole reservation to spend; one envelope comes back, attributed to the wrapper's adapter id and to no constituent model; the run-failure surface shows one phase; the cost row shows one composite; no constituent model has a worktree, a permission session, a route provenance record, a retained turn, or a name in the journal; a crash mid-fusion is unrecoverable because the reservation is not kind: "single"; and the factory says plainly, in the adapter's own source comment, that it supervises nothing inside the process it started. That is a real and honest promise. It is much smaller than the source record implied, and it is the one (b) could keep.

Why it is refused anyway. There is nothing to launch. Neither generation exposes a non-interactive entry, so the first task under (b) is not in this repository at all. And the promise, even if the entry point existed, would be bought against v1 — the generation with one architect and one builder, routed to claude:* and codex:*, which are the two providers AWSF already reaches natively. A two-model opaque composite that supervises nothing is strictly weaker than build-review, which is shipped, supervised and enabled today.

(a) Native — refused on cost, with the blockers named

(a) is buildable. It is not cheap, and the plan owes the list rather than the verdict. It needs: a fourth registration class and its verifier, because a composite child is neither a task edge nor a phase in a compiled workflow and the three existing classes each bind to one of those; a third barrier settlement mode carrying a per-child amount down to settle(id, spent), because the ReservationLedger seam has no method that takes one; a composite branch in restoreReservation, because a composite reservation cannot be recovered today; an orchestration layer that fans out, collects, and fuses; and a second spawn discipline living beside the broker's, which is the invariant-3 risk this plan is most wary of.

And the payoff for all of it is: a registration, a reservation, a worktree, a permission session and a retained envelope, per child. Which is the definition of a phase.

(c) Recipe — chosen

Fusion is a workflow. Each model consultation is a phase. compileWorkflow at compiler.ts:153 takes workflow: WorkflowDefinition — { id, phases: readonly PhaseDefinition[] } — and maps over the array; it never consults the shipped catalogue to do it, so a phase list built at runtime is structurally supported. A phase already carries an agent-phase broker registration with its own phaseId and phaseOrdinal, its own cost-1 reservation reserved at production-run.ts's phase loop, a permission session from its role's tool profile, a retained envelope, route provenance, a cost row, and an entry on the run-failure surface. Nothing in the list above needs to be built. It needs to be arranged.

Invariant 3 is untouched: (c) creates no spawn site, because a fusion phase launches through the existing agent-phase path that every shipped recipe already uses.

And what (c) gets for nothing

  • The per-role worktree recommendation is dropped, and the writer lease is not needed either. v2 replaced N worktrees with a filesystem lock keyed by the canonical checkout path, so exactly one shared-checkout writer exists at a time and every other agent is tool-enforced read-only. Its own header states it plainly: "Collaboration serializes every write-enabled task through one shared-CWD writer token; no worktrees." That is materially cheaper than N worktrees and it is implemented and shipping — and under (c) AWSF needs neither, because phases never run concurrently. Single-writer is structural here, not leased. The lease exists in v2 because several children run at once; AWSF's phase loop awaits each turn.
  • "May an opinion propose a diff?" is answered with evidence rather than preference. No. An opinion phase's role declares writes: [] and a readonly tool profile — the exact shape the shipped planner and reviewer roles already use — and evaluatePathPolicy returns outside-write-globs for every path when writes is empty. Its envelope schema is not awsf.build-output/v1, so it cannot produce a build claim the gates would accept. This maps cleanly onto the tiers exactly as the spine expected: opinion and debate phases are T0-shaped by blast radius, and only the fusing phase writes.
  • W17's hard structural blocker does not apply here. W17's Q1 is reviewBuildPhaseId's exactly-one-build-producer rule, which a five-ticket shift violates with five build phases. A fusion recipe has exactly one: the fuser. The opinion phases carry an opinion schema. W10 avoids that blocker by contract choice, not by amendment.
  • The host composer is a pattern, not an invention. The fuser needs N opinions and renderPrompt(previousEnvelope) hands a phase exactly one. build-review already solves this with review-context, a kind: "code" host phase that composes several phases' outputs into one envelope and adds no agent call and no ceiling movement. design-context and plan-context do the same. An opinion-context phase is the fourth instance of a shipped shape.

Solution

A fusion is a compiled recipe of N + 2 phases: N read-only opinion phases, one host composer that costs nothing, and one fusing phase that writes. Optionally a review phase after it, inheriting whatever the router decides. Each opinion phase holds its own cost-1 reservation, crosses the broker with its own agent-phase registration and ordinal, and returns a retained envelope attributed to the provider that answered.

The tier, the ceiling, and the arithmetic

A fusion recipe is T2. Not because its blast radius demands it — the opinion phases are read-only and T0-shaped — but because the tier bounds cost and blast radius together, and T0's own source comment defines it as "one call and no second opinion". An N-way opinion round is precisely a second opinion, so raising T0's ceiling to buy one would delete the sentence that defines the tier for every T0 recipe in the catalogue.

No code change to MAX_CALL_CEILING or ceilingFor is required by this workstream, at any N from 2 to 5. MAX_CALL_CEILING is 20 and caps the configured dial and the owner grant alike; ceilingFor(tier, resolved) already answers from whatever ceiling the caller resolved, including an awsf raise grant. What must allow it is the configured risk.call_ceiling.T2, which ships at 5.

Review independence once several providers have touched the work

The spine's constraint is routing.review: invert-provider, and the question it poses is sharp: may a provider that gave an opinion still review the fused result? This plan's answer has two halves, and only the first is W10's to decide.

The half W10 decides: independence is preserved by inverting against the fuser, not against the opinions. The rule's real purpose is at production-run.ts:1158, where the worker provider is read off the recipe's single build producer and the reviewer is selected by exclusion from it. Under (c) the single build producer is the fusing phase. So the reviewer must run on the provider opposite the fuser. An opinion is an input the fuser consumed and may have discarded; it is not the authorship of the candidate. A provider that gave an opinion may still review, provided it is not the fuser — and stating it that way keeps the rule checkable rather than moving it to a stronger guarantee nobody can enforce.

The half W10 does not decide: what happens at three or more distinct providers. providerPairFrom refuses it, W16 § M4 already owns the decision, and this workstream inherits whatever the owner takes there. W10's own duty is narrower and it is a task below: make the refusal name the third provider and the phase it came from, so a fusion roster that cannot carry a review says which slot is the reason.

[] Milestone M6: Collaborate — the delegation plan, and the two-stage compile

The last and most expensive of the six, and the one whose earlier refusal this plan now withdraws. The stated objection was wrong. It said a delegation graph cannot become an ordered phase array without throwing away the parallelism that is the point — but AWSF's phase loop awaits every turn, so there is no parallelism to throw away. Flattening a validated graph to a topological order costs this factory nothing it could otherwise have had. The tool needs a graph because it runs tasks concurrently as dependencies clear; AWSF would run them one at a time either way.

The real obstacle is a different one, and it is solvable. The graph is not a function of configuration — it is a model's answer. The architect reads every agent's plan and proposes the delegation. So a single-stage compile would make the phase list depend on a provider turn, which breaks reproducibility: INV-5 requires a recipe rebuildable byte-for-byte from recorded bytes, and a provider is not recorded bytes until its envelope is journalled. Two stages fix it exactly, and Q9 settled them as two tasks. Stage one is a small recipe that produces the delegation plan as a typed envelope, journals it, and commits it as its candidate. Stage two is a separate task, seeded from that candidate, compiling from the committed blob — which is recorded bytes, at a SHA Git already addresses — so the digest holds and a resume rebuilds identically. It is the same manifest-to-recipe shape shift uses, with the manifest written by a model instead of read off tickets. The plan crosses as a Git object, never as an envelope: CandidateAdoptionEvidenceSchema pins sourceEvidenceCopied: Literal(false), and this route keeps that honest rather than amending it.

Q9 — Are a collaboration's two stages one task or two?

Decided 2026-09-15 — two tasks, two ceilings. The recommendation is taken, and one clause of its stated cost was wrong and is corrected here rather than carried into a task.

What was wrong. The recommendation said the delegation plan has to be addressable across tasks, "which the journal already makes possible". It does not. The one mechanism that crosses tasks is awsf seed / adopt, and it carries Git objects only: CandidateAdoptionEvidenceSchema pins sourceEvidenceCopied: Literal(false) and sourceApprovalsCopied: Literal(false), and its header states it "never copies a source request, envelope, gate row, approval, provider session, or attempt-private file" (core/src/contracts/candidate-adoption.ts:9). An envelope is precisely what does not cross, and the two literal-false fields exist to keep it that way.

What is true instead, and it is a better route. The delegation plan crosses as a committed artifact, not as an envelope. Stage one validates and journals the architect's envelope inside its own task, then the host writes the validated plan into the worktree and commits it as the candidate. seed carries that candidate SHA into task two exactly as designed — no contract change, no new mechanism, and sourceEvidenceCopied: false stays honest because nothing is copied: task two reads a committed file out of its own seeded tree. Reproducibility gets stronger, not weaker: stage two compiles from a blob at a known SHA, which is more durable than a journal row and already digest-addressable by Git.

And the owner act comes for free. seed.ts:39 refuses a non-interactive terminal outright — CandidateSeedRejected("requires an interactive owner terminal"). That is INV-6 already enforced by a shipped command, so the stop between the stages is structural rather than new code, and M6 asserts it rather than building it.

What the decision still costs. Two tasks means two ceilings, which is the point — with three planners and four delegated tasks, one task would need (3 + 1) + (4 + 1) = 9 calls against a shipped T2 ceiling of 5, and a single raise would have to cover both stages before either had run. It also means a stage-one plan the owner declines costs nothing further. The price is that a collaboration is no longer one lineage in the journal, so "which plan did this run execute" is answered by the seeded candidate SHA rather than by a task id — which M6 task 19 must prove rather than assume.

Superseded recommendation text: two tasks, two ceilings — with the addressability claim that this decision corrects.
