In Plain Language

This section explains the whole plan without technical vocabulary. Everything after it says the same things precisely, for the people — and machines — building it.

What this is about

The factory being built here can already do several separate things well: start a brand-new project from nothing, keep track of which projects exist and where they live, turn an idea into a reviewed plan, and carry a piece of work from plan to finished-and-published. Each of those was built and proved on its own. What nobody has built is the route — the single path a project walks from the first idea to something published, passing through five landmarks along the way.

That route has been walked exactly once, by hand, by one person, who then wrote down what happened in ordinary sentences. Nothing was kept. There is no saved copy of what came out at each landmark — only the description of it. And a description of a thing you built once is a memory, not a record. Memories smooth over the parts that were awkward.

Why now

The four capabilities this route strings together have all just finished. The route is the last piece of ordinary building work left before the factory's plain-English handbook can be written, because the handbook has to describe a route that has stopped changing. Doing it now, while what it strings together is fresh, is cheaper than doing it later.

There is also a cost to waiting that grows quietly. Every week the only record of that hand-run stays a memory, the memory gets a little more confident and a little less accurate.

What we are building

Think of the route as five stops on a rail line, and think of the thing being built as two separate jobs done in a strict order.

The first job is to ride the line once and take photographs at every stop. Not sketches from memory — photographs, saved where anyone can look at them later. That is the entire first stage of this work, and nothing else may start until the photographs exist. This matters more than it sounds. If you skip it and build the timetable first, you build a timetable around the stops you remember, and every test you write afterwards will agree with you, because you wrote both. That kind of agreement proves nothing at all.

The second job is to build the timetable against those photographs. What each stop hands to the next one, what has to be true before a train may leave, and — the part that gets forgotten — the fact that no train leaves on its own. A person decides to travel each leg. There is deliberately no mechanism anywhere that moves a project from one stop to the next by itself, and a good part of this work is proving that no such mechanism exists rather than merely saying so.

One more rule runs through all of it, and it is the rule this particular piece of work is most likely to break. A five-stop route reads naturally like a set of instructions somebody follows. It must not be one. Nothing on this route may work by a machine having read a document first, because on one of the two routes the factory uses, documents are switched off entirely and cannot be reached at all. So the route is built out of checkable handovers, never out of instructions.

How we know it worked

Four things will be observably true, and none of them costs anything to check.

First, the photographs are in the tree, and they were put there before any of the timetable existed — the order is visible in the record itself. Second, a throwaway project travels the whole line from one end to the other, using a stand-in for the expensive parts, so the check is free and can be run as often as anyone likes. Third, at each of the four gaps between stops, a test confirms that nothing moved on its own: the journey stopped, and stayed stopped, until a person acted. Fourth, a check confirms that no stop on the line depends on any document having been read, and that check has itself been seen to fail on purpose, because a safety check nobody has ever watched fail is decoration.

And what is deliberately not part of this: no new machinery. This work is a route through capabilities that already exist. If building it turns out to need a new part, that is a signal the part belongs to whichever piece of work owns it, and this plan says so and hands it over rather than quietly building it here.

Purpose

Turn four separately-proved capabilities into one route a project actually travels, and do it in the order that makes the route an observation rather than a design: capture the five stage outputs into the tree as fixtures first, then write the governed workflow against those fixtures.

The spine names this workstream's evidence base as the weakest of any executable workstream in v2 — one hand-run of the five stages, in prose — and names the capture pass as its cheapest unused upgrade. Both statements point the same way, and this plan's whole structure is that arrow: M1 is the capture pass, M2 through M6 consume what M1 landed, and no task in M2 through M6 may cite the prose.

Problem

The stage envelopes have been produced exactly once, by hand, as prose. There is no fixture, no schema, and no evidence in this repository of what a stage output actually looks like. specs/awsf-v2-candidates-fuse-version.md §8 records this as a named absence — "Ladder stage envelopes | Produced once, by hand, as prose | The capture pass that is #22's first task" — and it is the only executable workstream in v2 whose evidence row is a single unrecorded run.

The failure mode is specific and it is not "the design might be wrong". It is that a workflow designed against remembered prose closes a loop with itself. The envelopes get designed from the memory; the fixtures get written to match the designed envelopes; the tests pass against the fixtures. Every step agrees with the one before it, the suite goes green, and at no point did anything get compared against what actually came out of a stage. The green is real and it means nothing, because the observation was never made.

A second problem sits underneath the first and is easier to miss. The five stages are not enumerated anywhere a planning session is permitted to read. The spine's W11 block names the workstream and its constraints but never lists the stages. The intent's W11 row says "capture pass first, then the governed workflow" and stops there. The fused candidate record names the ladder six times and enumerates it none. The list exists in the frozen candidate record, which the authoring session was instructed not to open — and that instruction is correct, because a list lifted out of a frozen prose record is exactly the remembered-prose input the ordering rule refuses. So the stage list is not an input to this plan. It is the first thing M1 produces.

The consequence, stated plainly

This plan cannot name its own stages, and does not pretend to. Every section below refers to them positionally as S1 through S5. M1 task 1 fills in the names, the owners, the producing commands and the entry preconditions, and records that list as a fixture. Everything after M1 is written against the shape every stage has regardless of what it turns out to be: an entry precondition, an owner, a typed envelope out of the thirteen W05 already landed, an exit stop, and a captured output. That shape is knowable now. The names are not, and inventing them here would be the defect this workstream exists to avoid.

Solution

Two acts, in a strict order, with the boundary between them enforced by the commit record rather than by intention.

Act one — capture. Drive a throwaway project through the ladder by hand, against the four landed workstreams as they actually stand, on the zero-quota stub route. Record what came out. Scrub it per invariants 1 and 9, land it under core/test/fixtures/stages/, then attempt each capture against every schema id in ENVELOPE_SCHEMAS and record the fit or the exact violations. Nothing is designed in act one. Where a stage output fits no landed schema, the misfit is written up and routed to W05, which owns envelope schemas — it is not fixed here.

Act two — the governed workflow. Declare the stage contract as values read from those fixtures, build a pure resolver that answers which stage is this project at and what must the owner do next, prove the four inter-stage boundaries stop by asserting the absence of any advancing code path, fence the whole thing against skill reach on three legs, and drive all five stages end to end on the stub route with no provider contacted.

The Capture Pass

M1 is not preparation for the workstream. It is the workstream's first and highest-leverage task, and the spine says so twice: once under Scope, and its order and again under Cheapest unused upgrade, where the capture pass is named as the cheapest thing in the workstream that changes what gets built. Those are the same sentence pointed at from two directions.

What is captured, and what "captured" means

Three artifact families, all under core/test/fixtures/stages/:

FixtureHoldsProduced by
stages.jsonthe ordered list — exactly five entries, each with id, ordinal, owner, producer (the command or workflow id that produces it), granularity (task or phase, per Q4), and entryPreconditionT01, from the hand-run itself
S1.json … S5.jsoneach stage's output exactly as it left its producer, scrubbed per invariants 1 and 9 and with timestamps normalized to one fixed instantT01, landed by T02
schema-fit.jsonper stage: the schema id from ENVELOPE_SCHEMAS it validates against, or an explicit misfit carrying the closest id and its ValidationViolation[]T03, by driving parse-envelope.ts — never by reading the output and judging
Two things T01 must not do, both of which read as helpful

It must not synthesize a stage it could not run. A hand-written envelope for an unreachable stage is the remembered prose again, wearing JSON. Per Q5's decision the stage is recorded as a stated absence with the evidence that would close it — the idiom the fused record's §8 already uses for every other gap in this project — and the journey covers four of five with the fifth marked [f] and its blocker named.

It must not tidy an awkward output. The value of a capture is entirely in the parts that surprise. An output that is longer, messier, or differently shaped than expected is the finding; smoothing it produces a fixture that agrees with the memory, which is the closed loop from the Problem section reintroduced at the one point it was supposed to be broken.

Why the capture runs on the stub route

The stub adapter is a real provider process with no provider behind it — core/src/adapters/stub.ts, whose header records that every phase, gate, workflow and journey test in v1's plan runs on it, which is why those journeys cost zero quota. The capture is a capture of envelope shape and stage sequence, not of model quality, and shape is exactly what a scripted adapter reproduces faithfully. Running the capture live would spend quota to learn nothing this workstream needs.

The limit, stated where a reader of the fixtures will see it

A stub-route capture establishes what a stage's output is required to look like and what the host does with it. It establishes nothing about what a real model puts in those fields. The distinction matters at exactly one place — S-stages produced by an agent rather than the host — and the fixture header for each such stage says so in one line, so a later reader does not mistake a shape fixture for a content sample.

The Stops, and Why They Are Absences

The spine's acceptance row reads: "The explicit stopping points stop, provably" — and the word doing the work is provably, set against the alternative it names two rows earlier, "documented as stopping". A documented stop is a sentence. A proven stop is a test that would go red if the stop were removed. The two are only the same thing if you never remove the stop.

There are four boundaries between five stages. Each one is held by exactly one of three mechanisms, all of which already exist:

MechanismWhat holds the boundaryHow the test observes it
Terminal-state sealthe earlier stage's task reaches a state from which LEGAL_EDGES offers no edge that could begin the next stageenumerate LEGAL_EDGES from the terminal state and assert the set contains no edge whose spawnSite is true
AWAITING_OWNER stopthe host advances into AWAITING_OWNER and every edge out of it carries actor human or owner — L19, L20, L22, L25assert the stop was reached, then assert no timer, no scheduled callback and no automatic resume exists — the posture W07 already established
Absence of any path at allthe earlier stage ends as a host command returning, and no code in core/src starts the next stage's producera static scan in the idiom of no-land-route.test.ts, plus the behavioural triple below

The behavioural observation is the same at every boundary and it is an absence triple: after the earlier stage completes, no new session row exists in the projection, no new attempt directory exists on disk, and no provider process was launched. The third is observed with the RouteLog idiom from core/test/journeys/t2-production.test.ts, which records every provider actually contacted so "no substitute ran" is observed rather than assumed.

Why an absence is the stronger guarantee here, and what it costs

A new lifecycle state — AWAITING_STAGE, or similar — would be a declaration that the project is between stages. It would also be an owner-authored commit into core/src/state/**, a migration into the protected migrations directory, and a widening of the lifecycle matrix, which W08 has just demonstrated costs a milestone and two gates. An absence costs none of that and asserts something a state cannot: not that the project is waiting, but that nothing anywhere is capable of stopping it waiting.

The cost is real and belongs here: an absence is invisible in the projection. A reader of the dashboard sees a completed task and nothing that says "and now a person acts". Q2's readout is what answers that, and it answers it by reading the journal rather than by writing a new state into it.

No Skill In The Path

The spine names this workstream as the one most likely to break the standing constraint, and gives the reason: "a five-stage ladder reads naturally as a document someone follows." The constraint is not a style preference. pi-codex launches with --no-skills and four sibling clean-room flags, so a phase running on that route cannot reach an installed skill at all — a stage that depended on one would not be poorly-designed, it would be broken on half the routes the factory uses.

What the repository already proves, and precisely what it does not

core/test/unit/meta/execution-isolation.test.ts holds two assertions and its header is careful about what each one buys. It pins --no-skills in the pi descriptor test's exact argv order, and it asserts .claude/ does not exist at the repository root — reading the filesystem, because absence is the control. The header then states the asymmetry outright: "claude-code has no verified equivalent … passes no flag that is known to disable project skill or command discovery."

What no test in this repository currently covers is a recipe or a gate reaching for a skill. Both existing assertions are about the launch surface. Neither says anything about whether a phase's prompt tells a model to invoke one, or whether a gate's verdict depends on a file having been read. That gap is this workstream's to close, and closing it with a test is not building a mechanism — so it stays here rather than being routed away.

The third leg is the one that matters, and it is the one a reviewer will want to cut

Legs one and two catch a stage that asks for a skill. Leg three catches the failure the constraint is actually written against: a gate that passes because a document said the right thing. That shape does not mention skills anywhere, imports nothing unusual, and reads as ordinary evidence-gathering. It is also the only one of the three that could plausibly be introduced by a well-meaning task in M2. Cutting leg three leaves the constraint asserted at exactly the two places nobody was going to break it.

All three legs are proven to bite in T16 by inducing one violation each in a throwaway and watching the suite go red. A fence nobody has seen fail is decoration — the rule this repository already applied when it proved the plan-aware sync fence with a ghost ticket directory.

Identifier Spine

This workstream's claims, declared here so W05's coverage gate can join them to the tasks that serve them. Ids are unique per plan, per the spine's decided Q5; a cross-plan reference would be written <plan-stem>#INV-n.

INV-1
The stage list is evidence, not memory. Every stage name, ordinal, owner, producer and entry precondition asserted anywhere in core/src is equal to core/test/fixtures/stages/stages.json, and that fixture was produced by a run rather than transcribed from prose. Neither frozen candidate record is read by this plan or by any of its tasks.
INV-2
ENVELOPE_SCHEMAS gains no entry from this workstream. Every stage output either validates against a schema id W05 already registered, or is recorded as an explicit misfit carrying at least one ValidationViolation and routed to W05 — never force-fitted, and never registered here.
INV-3
No stage reaches an installed skill and no gate this workstream declares reaches its verdict by reading a file. Asserted on three legs — composed prompt bytes, transitive import reach, and gate-closure inputs — each proven to go red on an induced violation.
INV-4
Nothing advances a project from one stage to the next. No code path in core/src starts stage N+1 because stage N finished, no timer resumes one, and after any stage completes there is no new session row, no new attempt directory and no launched provider until a person acts.
INV-5
The resolver answers and never acts. It is a pure function of resolved-project values and journal rows: no spawn, no write, no lifecycle transition, no filesystem reach, and no import from core/src/execution/**.
INV-6
This workstream adds no mechanism. Its diff touches no path matched by policy.protected_paths, it introduces no lifecycle state, no legal edge, no migration, no catalog field and no envelope schema, and it completes with no owner-authored commit. A task that cannot finish without one has found a routed mechanism and says so instead of widening.
AC-1
Five captured stage outputs and one stage list are in the tree before any file under core/src/stages/ exists. Observed in the commit record — M1's commits precede M2's — and by the fixtures test passing with the module directory absent.
AC-2
Every capture is scrubbed: no session id, attempt id, run id, absolute machine path or credential-shaped value survives, and timestamps are normalized to one fixed instant. Observed by reusing CREDENTIAL_PATTERNS and the existing absolute-path predicate rather than a second copy of either.
AC-3

No stage is recorded as fitting a schema it does not validate against, and the table is produced by driving the parser rather than by reading and judging.
AC-4

It is the answer to the real cost of INV-4: an absence is invisible in the projection, so without a readout a stopped project looks identical to a finished one.

The third option is presented so the trade is visible, not because it is viable. A command that advances a project would be the mechanism INV-4 asserts does not exist. Choosing it does not merely add a command — it deletes M3, changes what the workstream proves, and puts the standing constraint's most likely violation directly into the command surface.

DECIDED Q3 — What is an inter-stage stop addressed to, and how does the owner see it?

Decided 2026-08-26: keep the stop as an absence; Q2's readout names the act. INV-4 stands as written — no new lifecycle state, no per-project awaiting record, no owner-authored commit, and therefore no migration. M3 proves an absence rather than asserting a state. Paired with Q2, the pair is coherent: the absence is the guarantee and the readout is the legibility, and neither is asked to do the other's job.

Within a task, W07 settled this: the host stops at AWAITING_OWNER, with no timer and no automatic resume. Between stages there is no state at all — the earlier task is terminal and the later one does not exist yet. So the stop is real but unaddressed, and nothing in the projection says "a person acts next".

Recommended: keep the stop as an absence and let Q2's readout name the act. The absence is the stronger guarantee — it says nothing is capable of advancing, which a state cannot say — and the readout supplies the legibility the absence costs.

What option B costs, priced from W08: a per-project awaiting record is a new lifecycle state. W08 has just paid that bill — one owner-authored commit into core/src/state/**, one migration into the protected migrations directory, a widened lifecycle matrix, and a whole milestone. It also introduces a second source of truth for something the journal already knows, which is the reason W04's catalog was kept out of this in the first place.

DECIDED Q4 — Is a stage a whole task, or phases inside one task?

Decided 2026-08-26: mixed, with granularity recorded per stage. granularity is a required field on every entry in stages.json, and T01 fills it from what the run shows. There is no default and no fallback: a capture that cannot say whether a stage was a task or a set of phases is an incomplete capture, not a stage to guess about. Three different granularities across five stages is an expected result and must not be smoothed.

The five stages are probably not uniform, and the code says so. W05's design-to-plan recipe runs seven phases — request, design-context, design, architecture-review, plan-context, plan, plan-render — inside one task at tier 1. v1's simple-sdlc runs plan/build/test/document inside another. But awsf init is a host command with no task at all, and publication is a separate human-initiated act on an already-landed revision. Those are three different granularities before the capture pass has run.

Recommended: mixed, with granularity recorded per stage as a field in stages.json. It is the only option consistent with capture-first: the run reports the granularity, the fixture records it, and the contract carries it. Deciding uniformity in advance is designing the answer M1 exists to observe.

Why option C is refused rather than offered neutrally: one recipe spanning all five stages would have to span awsf init, which is host-only and runs before a repository exists. There is no execution context for such a recipe, and inventing one is the pre-repository execution mode W03 was designed specifically to not need.

DECIDED Q5 — What does the capture pass do with a stage it cannot run?

The argument for option B, stated fairly: a partially-built ladder is a ladder that cannot be walked, and M5's journey is the workstream's stop-when condition. DECIDED Q7 — Where do the captures live, and exactly what is scrubbed out of them?

Decided 2026-08-26: core/test/fixtures/stages/, full scrub, timestamps normalized rather than dropped. Out go session ids, attempt ids, run ids, real task ids, absolute machine paths, and anything matching CREDENTIAL_PATTERNS. Timestamp fields are kept and their values set to one fixed instant, so a stage output whose shape includes a timestamp stays a faithful shape sample. This is the one edit T02 is permitted to make to a capture, it is recorded in each fixture's header, and invariant 1 is the reason it outranks T01's rule against tidying.

Recommended: core/test/fixtures/stages/, with a full scrub. W07's core/test/fixtures/quota-axi/ is the precedent for a scrubbed capture in this tree, invariant 10's junk-drawer fence permits a .json there, and a fixture under specs/ would be a document rather than something a test loads.

The scrub rule, stated so it can be argued with: out go session ids, attempt ids, run ids, real task ids, absolute machine paths, and anything matching CREDENTIAL_PATTERNS. Timestamps are normalized to one fixed instant rather than removed, because a stage output whose shape includes a timestamp field must keep the field to stay a faithful shape sample.

Where the judgement call actually is: normalizing a timestamp is a small edit to a capture, and this plan's own M1 says not to tidy captures. DECIDED Q8 — How wide is the fence's third leg?

Decided 2026-08-26: this workstream's gates only, and leg three is built. The option to drop it was refused. T15 is not optional and is not a stretch goal — it is the leg that catches a gate passing because a document said the right thing, which is the failure the standing constraint is actually written against.

Leg three asserts that no gate reaches its verdict by reading a file. Some existing gates legitimately read the repository — the diff gates and the command-evidence gate observe the worktree, which is their job — so a fence over all gates would fail on day one for a reason that is not a defect.

Recommended: scope it to the gates this workstream's stage contract names. Narrow, true, and enforceable from the first commit. The limit gets written into the test header rather than left implicit, so nobody later reads a green fence as a claim about all gates.

Do not choose option C. Legs one and two catch a stage that asks for a skill. Why the stage module sits outside core/src/workflow/

core/test/unit/meta/loc-budget.test.ts gives core/src/workflow an advisory ceiling of 800 logical lines and every file a ceiling of 450. That is advisory and would not fail a run, so it is not the reason. The reason is structural: everything under core/src/workflow/ runs inside a task — recipes, phases, gates, corrections, the engine. The stage module is the opposite thing. It reasons across tasks, it runs no phase, and per INV-5 it never acts. Putting it under workflow/ would place a module that must not execute anything inside the directory whose entire contents execute.

Three things this plan deliberately does not build

  • A stage field in the catalog. ProjectCatalogSchema is additionalProperties: false and awsf.project.yaml is a protected path, so a stage field is an owner commit plus a W04 schema change. It is also a second source of truth for something the journal already records, which is the objection W04's own scope cut was written against. Stage position is derived, always.
  • A ladder recipe. A WorkflowRecipe runs inside one task. Under Q4's recommended answer at least one stage spans a whole task and at least one runs before any task exists, so a single recipe cannot express the ladder without inventing an execution context that W03 was specifically designed not to need.
  • A dashboard surface. W02 owns evidence readability and no-land-route.test.ts already bars lifecycle-write routes from the dashboard. A stage panel would be new surface with a different owner, and Q2's terminal readout answers the same need inside this workstream's boundary.

Risks this plan carries knowingly

  • R1 — the capture gets tidied. The single highest risk in the workstream, and it produces no error anywhere. A capture smoothed to match expectations reintroduces the closed loop at the exact point the ordering was supposed to break it. The mitigation is instruction rather than mechanism, stated in T01 and repeated in the ticket README, and it is honest to say that no test catches this one.
  • R2 — the contract drifts from the fixture. A later session renames a stage in contract.ts for clarity and does not touch stages.json. T09's equality test closes this, and T09 requires the drift to be induced and observed before the milestone exits.
  • R3 — leg three gets cut in review as over-engineering. It is the leg with the least obvious payoff and the most implementation cost, and it is the only one that catches the failure mode the standing constraint actually names. Q8 exists so the cut is a decision rather than a drift.
  • R4 — a stage needs something this workstream may not build. Handled by construction: the routing table in What This Consumes names the owner for each case, and Q6 bounds how much the block stops. The residual risk is that a stage needs a mechanism owned by nothing — a resident advancing process, or a document a gate must read — in which case the finding is that the ladder as remembered is not buildable under v2's constraints, and that finding belongs to the owner rather than to a task.
  • R5 — the journey grows slow. Five stages in one temporary directory is the longest journey in the repository. v1's largest is 1012 lines. A journey nobody runs is a journey that is not protecting anything, which is why T19 records its runtime rather than only its verdict.

What would change this plan

  • M1 finding fewer or more than five stages. The spine says five and the hand-run reported five; if the capture reports four or six, the fixture wins and the plan is amended. stages.json is the source of truth and the number five is not load-bearing anywhere except in the plan's title, which is the spine's name for the workstream rather than a claim about the code.
  • Every capture fitting a landed schema on the first pass. Then INV-2 is satisfied trivially, T04 becomes a one-line record, and the workstream's strongest result is that "adds no new mechanism" was true rather than aspirational. Record it as a result.
  • Two stages turning out to be one. If the capture shows two of the five have no boundary between them — no owner act, no stop — then there are four boundaries claimed and three that exist, and M3 shrinks. That is a finding about the ladder, not an error in the capture.
  • The readout needing something the journal does not hold. Q2 is decided and awsf stage is committed, but the resolver's entire input surface is queries.ts. If answering "where is this project" turns out to need a fact the journal never recorded, that is a finding for W02 or W04 rather than a reason to write a new record — and it would be the first real test of whether stage position is genuinely derivable.
