In Plain Language

This section explains the whole plan without technical vocabulary. Everything after it says the same things precisely, for the people — and machines — building it.

What this is about

The owner runs a workshop where automated workers build software overnight. Several times a night the work reaches a point where only the owner may decide what happens next: the job has used the budget it was given, the monthly allowance of the hired machines has run low for a few hours, a checker asked for changes, or a step failed. Today the workshop simply stops at that point and waits. If the owner is asleep or away, nothing happens until they return, even when the right answer was obvious and the owner would have given it in five seconds.

Why now

The workshop now runs long, multi-part jobs by itself, and the owner plans to run many more of them while away. A new kind of tool has also appeared: a very small, very cheap judge that answers yes-or-no and pick-one questions in about a second for a tiny fraction of a cent. It cannot write or do anything; it only answers. The owner tried it once on the kind of question the workshop stops on, and it gave a sensible, cautious answer for three thousandths of a cent.

What we are building

Think of a trusted house-sitter with a written note from the owner. Before leaving, the owner writes the note for one job only: "you may add up to this much budget, you may wait for the allowance to refill and then continue, you may send work back once for a fix, you may start a follow-up job twice, and you may stop the job if it is going in circles." The house-sitter checks the facts at each stop, asks the cheap judge a few quick questions, follows fixed rules, and does only what the note allows. They never sign anything in the owner's name: every action is written in the logbook as "done by the house-sitter, under note number such-and-such". They can never hand the finished work over for release, never vouch that it works, and never write themselves a new note. For the first weeks the house-sitter mostly watches and writes down what they would have done, so the owner can compare it with what they actually did; an action becomes routine once it has matched the owner's own choice seven times out of eight, unless the owner says "do it all, on my responsibility". The same cheap judge also helps the workers themselves: it warns before a dangerous command, flags text that tries to give the workers orders, tells the owner's assistant when to tidy its memory, and answers questions about files so workers do not have to read them in full.

How we know it worked

A job left running overnight is still moving in the morning instead of parked at its first stop; the logbook shows every stop, what the house-sitter proposed, what it did and why, and nothing it did is recorded as the owner's; the owner can switch it off for a job at any moment and it stops before its next action; and a page in the workshop's dashboard shows, over weeks, how many hours of waiting it saved, how often it agreed with the owner, how often the owner had to undo it, and what the cheap judge cost. Deliberately not part of this: releasing work, vouching for work, fixing code, or anything the note does not name.

Purpose

W19 adds two things to AWSF. The first is a decision service: one host-side transport to Jev, TypeSafe's System One classifier reached through OpenRouter, with versioned question sets, pure policies and one journaled record per call. The second is the Delegate: host code that, under an owner-granted lease scoped to one task, takes a closed set of owner acts at the stops a run makes, so a shift keeps running while the owner is away. Around them it builds the rest of the ladder the owner asked for (J1–J9): guardrail signals inside agent phases, a compaction judgment for Marimba, cheap-read tools that answer questions about files without reading them into an agent's context, and a metrics view that shows over time whether any of it helps.

The line the factory has always drawn holds: the host advances lifecycle state, models supply structured facts, and a human authorises anything that lands. Jev is a model supplying facts. The Delegate is host code acting on the owner's written, revocable authority. Neither lands, publishes or attests anything.

Problem

An overnight shift can stop at five kinds of point, and every one of them waits for an owner act that the guard denies to Marimba by verb (OWNER_ACTS in docs/driving/marimba/marimba-guard-rules.mts: land, cancel, rework, review, degrade-review, attribute, journey, raise, publish, resume, grant). awsf seed also requires an interactive owner terminal. Five facts shape the design:

  • Jev is a classifier, not an agent. One live call on 2026-09-28 (the owner's authorized smoke, synthetic state, through the reference repository's own client): POST https://openrouter.ai/api/alpha/decisions, ~typesafe/jev-latest resolved to typesafe/jev-1.13-20260917, 1,172 ms cold, 670 input and 88 output tokens, reported cost $0.00002814, which is exactly $0.042 per million input tokens with output free. Asked about a ceiling stop in a passing shift, it picked raise at 0.75 with confidence 0.68 and scored risk 0.97 of 2 — a pick a 0.85 bar turns into "wait". It cannot write text, run commands or hold an identity.
  • The quota arithmetic already exists as numbers. quota-axi 0.1.29 reports, per provider, a five_hour and a seven_day window with resetsAt, percentRemaining and a pace reserve (Claude at 21:34Z: 31% of the five-hour window, resetting 22:20Z; 61% of the week). This is what the owner reads by hand before resuming, and it belongs in code, not in Jev.
  • No model output may move a task. DETERMINISTIC_REASON_SOURCES in core/src/state/errors.ts admits six sources for a BLOCKED reason — process, exit code, git, gate, record, human — "no seventh". AWAITING_OWNER has no timeout by design.
  • Most roles are not on pi. awsf.config.yaml runs planner, reviewer, documenter and scout on the claude adapter and the builder on codex (pi). pi children run with --no-extensions, which the adapter documents as essential. Nothing in core/src calls fetch today, and the adapter fence forbids it in adapters.
  • The reference code needs changes before AWSF can use it. Its jev-guard.ts catches every error and lets the call through; its ask_jev runs commands with exec and a shell string (invariants 3 and 4); its write gate was bypassed in a live run by a bash heredoc (its own README); its direct TypeSafe transport is untested. Its thresholds are labeled demonstrations.

Solution

Seventeen design decisions, each owned by one task. Authority grows in one direction only: a Jev answer can always make the factory stop or ask; it can make the factory do more only under an owner lease.

DDDecisionTask
DD1One transport, core/src/decision/jev-transport.ts: OpenRouter only, the moving alias ~typesafe/jev-latest with the resolved model recorded on every call (Q7), strict response validation ported from the reference client, redaction before egress, a per-project switch, and a fence that keeps it the only fetch in core/src.1
DD2Every question is a versioned question set: questions, thresholds and a pure policy in one file, as the reference repository keeps them. Jev never does arithmetic.2
DD3Every call appends one awsf.decision/v1 record to the task's decisions.jsonl, projected as an events row. No migration; a rebuild reproduces it.2
DD4At every stop the host builds one fact record from the status and the journal. No prose except the reviewer's verbatim findings.4
DD5The shadow owner is always on: every stop journals the proposal the Delegate would make, with or without a lease. Numbers decide first; Jev only refines judgment.5
DD6Agreement is computed at read time by pairing each proposal with the next act on that attempt. Code-decided acts are live from the first lease; judgment acts unlock at 7 of their last 8 proposals matched; the owner's autonomy all switch unlocks everything for one lease and is labeled owner-all (Q8).6
DD7One authority type for every delegable command: owner (the interactive terminal, unchanged) or delegate (a lease check). The delegate actor is added only to the task-machine edges the delegable acts are traced to.8
DD8The lease is a task-scoped, append-only record granted at the owner's terminal: acts, caps, autonomy, fallback routes, an optional end time. Continuations inherit it. Revocation is one command.9
DD9The quota plan is arithmetic over the five-hour and weekly windows and an expected draw from past boundary snapshots, labeled planning-only and never shown as a task's cost.10
DD10With a lease, awsf run hands each stop to the Delegate in the same foreground process instead of exiting. It waits in-process for a reset (Q3a), recovers from the journal when restarted, and hands back everything it may not do. A notify hook runs an owner-configured argv through the broker (Q10).11
DD11Judgment acts: rework with a finding Jev picks and code copies verbatim (one per attempt); replacement review only on host eligibility; degrade-review only when the lease names it.13
DD12Route fallback (Q3c): a leased route per role, applied as a journaled override the resolver reads like --route. The resolver never reads quota; the quota fence stays true.14
DD13Continuation: from a ticket block or an admitted BLOCKED code, Jev classifies the failure, a code table decides, and the Delegate starts a seeded follow-up task. The shift still never retries itself.15
DD14Agent phases reach Jev only through a host socket, so the key never enters an agent's environment and every call is journaled by the host.17
DD15Guard signals are extra: when Jev is unavailable, today's fences stay the control and the phase continues (Q11). The reference's secret write gate is dropped because redaction removes what it would judge.18
DD16Marimba's compaction judgment runs in the owner's own pi session and calls the transport directly; it is not an AWSF phase.21
DD17Every metric is read-time over the journal, carries the resolved Jev model and question-set version, and labels estimates as estimates.26

Identifier Spine

Identifiers are unique within this plan. Every declaration is served by at least one task, and each ticket's serves mirrors its task's set; the invariant-12 fence compares them.

INV-1
Authority, never identity. Every Delegate act is journaled with actor: delegate, its lease id, its decision ids and its autonomy basis. No record written under delegate authority names the owner or a human, and commit identities are unchanged (invariant 11).
INV-2
Jev supplies facts; code decides. A Jev answer never moves lifecycle state by itself and is never a BLOCKED reason source. Every act passes a pure policy table whose numbers come from code, then the lease check.
INV-3
A closed act set. The Delegate can never land, publish, attest a journey, grant protected content, attribute, or grant or extend its own lease. The authority type cannot express those acts, and a fence proves the commands that perform them never accept it.
INV-4
One transport, no credential in a phase. Only core/src/decision/jev-transport.ts calls the Jev endpoint; redaction runs before egress; agent phases reach Jev through the host socket and never receive the key; a project switch turns it off.
INV-5
Safe by direction. When Jev or quota data is unavailable, extra signals fall back to today's fences and the phase continues, and Delegate acts fall back to handing the stop to the owner.
INV-6
Lease-bounded. Every Delegate act is inside one owner-granted, task-scoped, revocable lease with caps (ceiling ≤ 20, continuations ≤ 2, reworks ≤ 1, a Jev spend cap). The lease is re-read before every act and after every wait, and it ends when its task lands, is cancelled, or hits a stop it does not cover.
INV-7
The journal is the only evidence store. Decision, proposal, lease and act records are journaled and projected by the existing projector; every metric is computed at read time; fixtures are synthetic; no committed file holds runtime data or matches /manifest|receipt/i.
AC-1
A typed Jev call goes through one fenced transport with redaction, a project switch, strict validation, the resolved model and a cost with its source, and leaves one journaled decision record that a rebuild reproduces.
AC-2
Every stop a run makes journals a shadow proposal built from host facts, and a replay seeds the record from the owner's past stops.
AC-3
Agreement with the owner is computed at read time; judgment acts unlock at 7 of 8 matched; the owner can switch a lease to owner-all and back, and every act names its basis.
AC-4
An owner grants, inspects and revokes a lease at their terminal; under it a run raises and resumes at a ceiling, waits for and resumes after a quota reset per the quota plan, cancels a looping attempt, notifies the owner, and hands back everything else.
AC-5
Under a lease, rework with a verbatim finding, replacement review on host eligibility, degrade-review when named, and a leased fallback route all run only with earned or owner-all autonomy, and the resolver never reads quota.
AC-6
A ticket block or an admitted BLOCKED code becomes at most two seeded continuation tasks under the same lease; any other code hands back.
AC-7
pi-route phases get a bash gate, a result screen and a reviewer-injection row through the host socket; claude-route phases get the same if the proof holds; every failure falls back to today's fences.
AC-8
Marimba's pi session gets a compaction judgment with tiers and a cut point, and the owner's pilot is recorded against the 2026-09-24 baseline.
AC-9
Every configured role the tool path reaches can ask Jev about files and about its own state, paths and a screened argv command, without the content entering its context.
AC-10
The metrics tab gains a Delegate view with waiting avoided, agreement, reversals, stops by kind, Jev spend and bytes not read, filterable by Jev model version.

Execution: manual first, then shifts under W19's own Delegate

M1 to M3 run manually, one ticket per session, as W18 does since 2026-09-28. They build the authority every later shift runs under, and T08 and T09 write protected paths as owner gates, which no shift builder can reach. M4 to M8 run as one AWSF shift per milestone, normally under a lease the owner grants before leaving, so W19 is the first real user of what it builds. A milestone lands before the next starts: awsf start bases the worktree on HEAD. A shift of N tickets costs N + 1 calls against the T2 ceiling of 5.

Proposed text, for the owner to edit before committing:

13. **The Delegate acts only under an owner lease, and never as the owner.** An owner act
    runs from the owner's interactive terminal, or under an owner-granted, task-scoped,
    revocable lease that names it. Delegated acts are journaled as `actor: delegate` with
    their lease and decision ids. The Delegate never lands, publishes, attests a journey,
    grants protected content, attributes, or grants its own lease. A Jev answer is never a
    BLOCKED reason source and never moves lifecycle state by itself. Only

Questionables

The owner decided Q1–Q15 on 2026-09-28 (Decisions already taken). Authoring surfaced five more, numbered on from there. All five were decided on 2026-09-28 by owner review, each as recommended, so the tickets, which were written against those recommendations, stand unchanged.

DECIDEDQ16 — May Marimba start awsf run on a task that has a lease?

Decided 2026-09-28: Marimba may start a leased run, as recommended. No note.Recommendation: yes. The lease is the authority, and the owner granted it at their terminal; who presses start adds nothing to it. It also means a crashed run can be restarted by Marimba and recover from the journal (task 11). Marimba stays denied every awsf delegate verb, so it can never create, widen or switch a lease. Alternative: only the owner starts a leased run. Safer on paper, but a crash overnight then waits for the owner, which is the problem this plan exists to fix.

DECIDEDQ17 — How do agent phases reach Jev?

Decided 2026-09-28: the host socket, as recommended. No note.Recommendation: the host socket (task 17). The key never enters an agent's environment, every call is redacted and journaled in one place, and the lease's spend cap is enforced where the money is spent. Cost: a small node:net server per phase and a sandbox check. Alternative: a separate OpenRouter key with a small credit limit in each phase's environment. Simpler, but the reference repository records an agent printing its environment in a live run, and a printed credential-shaped string aborts AWSF's rework path.

DECIDEDQ18 — How much of each Jev request and response does the decision record keep?

Decided 2026-09-28: redacted bodies up to 16 KiB, else digests, as recommended. No note.Recommendation: the redacted bodies when together under 16 KiB, else their sha256 digests. A stop decision is about 700 tokens, so most records keep everything needed to reproduce and audit the answer, and a large file judgment keeps a digest. Alternatives: digests only (smallest journal, no audit of what Jev saw), or full bodies in a separate state-root file (complete audit, a second evidence store beside the journal that invariant 10's spirit discourages).

DECIDEDQ19 — If task 19's claude-route proof fails, what do "all roles" get?

Decided 2026-09-28: tools stay pi-only, as recommended. No note.Recommendation: the Jev tools and guard stay on pi-route roles (today the builder), task 19 closes [f] with its evidence, and W18's metrics decide separately whether any role should move to a pi route. Alternatives: move scout and reviewer to pi routes now so they get the tools (changes W18's comparisons and the review inversion's provider pairing), or hold M7 until a claude path exists (delays the token savings with no date).

DECIDEDQ20 — Does AWSF ship a default notify command?

Decided 2026-09-28: no default; the owner supplies an argv, as recommended. No note.Recommendation: no default. The placement layer takes any argv the owner chooses, and AWSF never picks a third-party service or sends a message by default. Alternative: a documented example (for instance a push-notification CLI) in the cheatsheet: faster setup, but it reads as an endorsement and needs a network service the factory does not otherwise use.

Ideas carried from the reference, and how

  • Numbers in code, judgment in Jev. Quota, ceilings, counts and dates never go to Jev (tasks 5, 10).
  • Confidence is a second axis. Every policy has a floor and a bar; below the bar the Delegate hands back (tasks 5, 13, 15).
  • Give the model an exit. Every choice has other, none or wait_for_owner.
  • Describe situations, not degrees. Score levels in every set are observable situations.
  • Fan out. A stop asks all its questions in one call.
  • A cheap filter before a strong model. ask_jev_files narrows, the agent reads only what survives (task 23).
  • A model inside a model. The compaction judgment watches Marimba's own context (task 21).
  • Think in "and", not "or". Jev adds to the fences, the review and the owner; it replaces none of them.
  • The only benchmark that matters is your own. Shadow agreement and the W18 tab, not the reference's demo thresholds.

Rejected while authoring

  • Jev as a phase in every run. A phase is an agent turn with an envelope; Jev writes nothing. It is a host service called at stops and hooks.
  • Jev taking the owner's identity. Authority, yes, under a lease; identity, never (invariant 11, INV-1).
  • An outside agent in another session fixing code mid-run. It would bypass every gate and the delegation fence. The in-factory form is the continuation task (task 15).
  • Marimba holding the lease authority. An agent that can be talked round would hold owner acts; the guard design exists to prevent exactly that.
  • A background daemon or OS scheduler to wake for a reset. The foreground run waits; a restart recovers from the journal (Q3a).
  • The reference's secret write gate. Redaction removes what it would judge; the credential-pattern fence already covers it.
  • A TypeSafe direct transport. Untested in the reference, and one provider keeps one fence simple.
  • Delegated landing. Watch-only (land-shadow). The owner wants to see a candidate before it lands, and "a human authorises anything that lands" is unchanged.

Positions this plan revises, by owner decision

  • Intent decision 5, "no timer, no automatic resume". Replaced within a lease by a journaled, lease-bound wait (task 11). Replacement guarantee: no wait without a lease, the lease is re-read after every wait, and a revoked lease ends the wait.
  • Intent decision 5, "quota is never a router". Replaced within a lease by a leased fallback route (task 14). Replacement guarantee: the resolver still never reads quota; the quota fence proves it.
  • Intent decision 5, "no per-task cost from an account-wide percentage". Kept for display and storage; the expected draw is planning-only (task 10).
  • W17: "rework is not the unattended recovery instrument; the shift never retries itself". One delegated rework per attempt, never retried after a credential abort; continuation is a new task, so the shift still never retries itself.

Risks

  • R1 Confidently wrong. Several answers about one state share a model and correlate. Mitigation: numbers first, bars, earned autonomy, and owner-all labeled on every act it enables.
  • R2 Data leaving the machine. Approved (D2). Mitigation: redaction before egress and a per-project switch.
  • R3 The moving alias. Chosen (Q7). Mitigation: the resolved model on every record and the jevModel facet.
  • R4 Steering through prose. A builder's text could try to talk the Delegate into an act. Mitigation: stop facts are host facts; the only prose is verbatim review findings, and the policy table, not Jev, decides.
  • R5 The lease becoming the default. Mitigation: one task at a time, no "remember", caps, and every act journaled.
  • R6 The machine sleeping. A foreground wait dies with the laptop. Mitigation: the journal records the wait, and a restarted run continues from it.

What would change this plan

  • pi refusing -e beside --no-extensions (task 18) or the sandbox refusing the socket (task 17): M5 and M7 need another path, and the plan is amended.
