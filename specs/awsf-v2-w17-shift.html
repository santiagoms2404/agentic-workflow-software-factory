In Plain Language

This section explains the whole plan without technical vocabulary. Everything after it says the same things precisely, for the people — and machines — building it.

What this is about

The owner has a workshop that builds software for him. He gives it a job, it works, and then it stops and waits for him to look at the result and sign it off. That waiting step is deliberate: nothing the workshop makes ever goes out the door without a person looking at it first. The trouble is that his work comes in lists — a batch of five or six small related jobs that have to be done in order, because each one builds on the last. Today he has to be present for every single one: start it, wait, look, sign, start the next. Five jobs is five evenings.

Why now

He pays a fixed monthly amount to two different suppliers for the work the machines do, and that allowance resets on a clock whether he uses it or not. Every night he is asleep, and every afternoon he is out, that allowance drains away unspent. He wants the workshop to work through a list while he is away and have the finished pile waiting when he comes back. What made this worth planning now rather than wishing for is that a previous look at the idea measured the ground carefully and wrote down what it found — and two of the things it found have since changed.

What we are building

The obvious way to do this is to queue up five jobs and let something start them one after another. That was tried and it does not work, for a reason built into the workshop's own rules: the sign-off step is a door only a person can open, and there is no key and no timer. So five jobs would stop at the first door and the other four would never start.

So this builds the opposite thing. Instead of five jobs, it makes one job with five stages inside it. The list of small jobs is read off the paperwork the owner has already written, turned into a running order, and handed to the workshop as a single instruction sheet. The work all happens on one bench, in one place, each stage adding to the last — so when the owner comes back there is one pile with five clearly separated layers in it, and one door to open instead of five. It also means the workshop only has to set the bench up once, which today takes long enough that setting it up five times would eat the whole night.

Three things follow from that shape without anyone having to decide them. The work accumulates in one place rather than five, so nothing has to be stitched together afterwards. There is one spending limit for the whole evening rather than five separate ones that add up to something nobody budgeted. And if stage three goes wrong, everything stops there — which is right, because stage four was going to be built on top of stage three, and building on something broken is worse than stopping.

How we know it worked

The owner points the workshop at one group of jobs, walks away, and comes back to a single finished pile with each job's work separately visible inside it, a written record of exactly which jobs ran and in what order, and one decision to make instead of five. If something went wrong partway, he finds it stopped at that point with everything before it kept and nothing after it started or paid for — and he can pick it up from there without redoing what already worked. What is deliberately not part of this: the workshop still cannot sign anything off by itself, still cannot send anything outside the building, and a person still has to be the one who starts the evening.

Purpose

shift is a new workflow type: a recipe whose phase list is compiled from a selected set of plan tickets at attempt-creation time, rather than declared statically in core/src/workflow/recipes/. One shift executes the tickets of one or more selected milestones in dependency order, unattended, inside one attempt, one worktree and one call ceiling, and reaches the owner exactly once.

It supersedes the “away mode” candidate recorded in the 2026-09-03 amendment of the v2 spine. That entry's measurements are taken as given and are not re-derived here; the sections below say where this plan departs from it and why, and two of the departures are because the ground moved after 2026-09-03.

Problem

The away-mode framing was a queue of independent runs. That shape cannot be unattended, and the reason is measured in the code rather than argued. core/src/state/task-machine.ts:115 declares L20 — the edge from AWAITING_OWNER to LANDING — as actors: ["human"], interactive: true. It is the only route into LANDED, there is no tier exemption anywhere in LEGAL_EDGES, and core/src/state/errors.ts:22 records that the absence of a clock from the deterministic reason sources is deliberate: “AWAITING_OWNER has no timeout, so no timer can produce L21 no matter what else it satisfies.”

A shift of nine independent attempts therefore halts on ticket 1 and waits for a person. The other eight never start. Unattended sequencing requires the tickets to be phases of ONE attempt, not nine attempts.

Three further costs follow from the many-attempts shape and disappear in the one-attempt shape, each of them measured:

  • Seeding is paid per attempt. runtime.seed_paths in awsf.config.yaml:24 is [node_modules], which on this repository is 139 MB (du -sh node_modules). Nine attempts is nine copies before the first provider call. The amendment named this as the practical blocker nobody had hit yet.
  • Budget is per task. CallBudget is constructed per task and callsSpent is task-lifetime. Nine attempts is nine independent ceilings and no total, so a batch can drain a subscription without any single attempt exceeding anything.
  • The candidate lands on no ref. createWorktree (core/src/git/worktrees.ts:267) runs git worktree add --detach. Every candidate this factory has produced is a dangling commit. Nine unattended runs produce nine commits git is entitled to collect before the owner wakes up.

Solution

A compiled recipe. compileWorkflow at core/src/workflow/compiler.ts:153 already takes a WorkflowDefinition — { id, phases: readonly PhaseDefinition[] } — and never reads the shipped catalogue to do it. Nothing in the compiler requires a phase list to be written by hand. All eight recipes in core/src/workflow/recipes/ are static today; shift is the first that is not.

How a ticket becomes phases. Each selected ticket contributes a fixed group of three:

PhaseKindSchemaProvider callsWhat it is
t<NN>-briefengineerawsf.plan-output/v10Host-executed. Reads the ticket file and emits the ticket's own ## Build prompt block as this segment's intent. The owner already wrote it; no model is needed to interpret it.
t<NN>-buildagentawsf.build-output/v11Owner builder, maxCorrections: 1. Produces this ticket's own host commit on the accumulating worktree head.
t<NN>-testscodeawsf.test-output/v10Host runs the configured gate commands against this ticket's candidate SHA, before the next ticket is allowed to start.

Then, once for the whole shift and only at the end: shift-review-context (code) and shift-review (agent, owner reviewer, one call) over the accumulated diff. So a shift of N tickets has minimumCalls = N + 1, and the build and review routes are the same two providers inversion already resolves.

Three of the amendment's seven open questions are answered by construction, not by preference — see Notes → What the shape decides on its own. A fourth follows from the single owner gate.

Identifier Spine

Per the v2 spine's Q5 decision, identifiers are unique within this plan and a cross-plan reference is qualified by plan stem. Every declaration below is served by at least one task, and each ticket's serves mirrors its task's as a set — the invariant-12 fence compares the two and fails when either is edited alone.

INV-1
Landing stays human and at a TTY. No shift phase, gate, record or readout may produce L20, and nothing a shift writes may become an input a timer could turn into L21. A shift runs up to the owner and stops.
INV-2
The dashboard records intent; a human-started session compiles and drains it. AGENTS.md invariant 3 keeps node:child_process out of every file but the transport broker, so the API server cannot spawn. No route added by this workstream may be anything but read-only.
INV-3
No push path and no skill in the execution path. AGENTS.md invariant 8 is untouched by this workstream, and a merge to a production branch stays a local act the owner authorises by hand.
INV-4
A shift's compiled recipe is reproducible. Recovery recompiles the phase list and compares a binding digest, so the recipe must be rebuildable byte-for-byte from the recorded manifest and the ticket files it names. A moved ticket byte refuses the resume rather than silently changing the run.
INV-5
A shift never widens its own ceiling. awsf raise stays an interactive owner act with a recorded reason, and no shift code path reaches it. A shift that exhausts its ceiling stops and says where it is.
AC-1
One selection act names a plan stem and one or more milestones; the host expands it to a single ordered ticket list spanning them, refuses every ambiguous expansion by name, and records the selection with a per-file byte digest so a reader can later see exactly which tickets ran.
AC-2
N tickets compile to a phase list whose minimumCalls is N + 1, admitted or refused against the attempt's ceiling at zero cost, before any process exists, with a refusal that names the shortfall in calls and in raise acts.
AC-3
One attempt, one worktree, one accumulating head. Each ticket's work is its own host commit, recorded against its ticket id, and ticket n builds on ticket n−1's tree in place.
AC-4
A failing ticket blocks the attempt at that ticket. Every earlier ticket's phase result is accepted and resumable, and no later ticket's provider call is reserved or spent.
AC-5
A sealed shift's candidate is reachable by a ref rather than by a reflog entry, and every ticket's own candidate SHA is recorded beside its ticket id.
AC-6
The shift reaches AWAITING_OWNER exactly once, and the owner readout names each ticket, its commit, its gate result and its review finding, over a candidate that was built fresh rather than read off a stale bundle.

Decision: the failure classifier reads shift, and shift does not read it

2026-09-20 — selection widened from one milestone to one or more, at the owner's direction

No status marker moved, and no code was written. Every task in this plan is still []; this amendment changes what three of them are authored to build, and the paired Section B blocks and tickets T01, T02 and T08 move with it in the same commit.

The decision

The owner asked that a shift be able to take several milestones in one selection, so that landing happens once at the end however many milestones are in the shift. As first authored, selection was plan + one milestone, and a run of three milestones meant three chained shifts and three owner gates.

Why this shape rather than chaining. Three chained shifts cannot run unattended end to end, and the reason is measured rather than argued: a shift is never a legal seed or adoption target. validateInheritedSeed (core/src/workflow/candidate-seed.ts:101–105) admits only build-review and simple-sdlc at tier 2, adopt.ts's SUPPORTED map says the same, and task 17 of this plan declines adopting into a shift deliberately. So a chained shift starts from the repository HEAD, and if milestone n+1 builds on milestone n's code, the owner has to land in between — which is a TTY act, and the night is over. Widening the selection removes that dependency instead of working around it, and it touches the selection surface rather than the seed invariant. Two alternatives were rejected: landing between milestones (no code change, but it is the interactive act this workstream exists to avoid), and admitting a shift as a seed target (it reverses task 17's decision and buys a second reconciliation surface between an inherited diff and a manifest).

What changes

  • AC-1 now reads “one or more milestones” and “a single ordered ticket list spanning them”.
  • Task 1 — the manifest. milestone: string becomes milestones: string[], minItems 1, in the owner's selection order. It is a list even when it holds one; there is no singular form and no one-milestone special case. Nothing is built yet, so this is an authoring change, not a schema migration.
  • Task 2 — the expander. selectShiftTickets(plan, milestones, records) returns one topological order over depends_on across the whole selection, tie-broken by the milestone's position in the list and then by ticket id.
  • The refusal that generalises, and it is the whole of the change. “A depends_on edge leaving the milestone” becomes “leaving the selection”. An edge from one selected milestone into another is no longer a refusal at all — it is inside the selection, and honouring it is what the single topological order is for. Three refusals are added: a repeated milestone id, a named milestone holding no tickets, and the existing empty-expansion case now measured over the whole selection.
  • No contiguity rule, deliberately. M1,M3 with M2 skipped is a legitimate selection when M3 does not depend on M2; when it does, the leaving-the-selection check already refuses it. A contiguity rule would refuse a correct selection, so the checklist says not to add one.
  • Task 8 — the CLI and the readout. --milestone takes a comma-separated list and may also be repeated. A range syntax is not added: one spelling, not two.
  • Task 3 — the tests. A three-milestone case, a cross-milestone edge honoured, and a non-contiguous selection accepted.

What does not change, and is worth saying so

  • One attempt, one worktree, one accumulating head, one owner gate. The shape this plan exists to establish is untouched — this widens what may be selected, not what a shift is.
  • minimumCalls = N + 1, with N now the total ticket count across the selection. The formula is unchanged; only the input grows.
  • Blocking semantics. A failing ticket still blocks the attempt at that ticket, with the prefix durable and the tail unreserved.
  • Task 17's chaining is not superseded. --continues and groupSessionStacks remain how separate shifts are joined into one readable stack. A multi-milestone selection reduces how often chaining is needed; it does not replace it.
  • A shift is still never a seed or adoption target. Task 17's refusal stands, and this amendment makes it less pressing rather than reopening it.

The bound this makes load-bearing

MAX_CALL_CEILING = 20 (core/src/state/tiers.ts:57) is in code, not config, and minimumCalls = N + 1. So 19 tickets is the hard maximum for one shift, however many milestones they span, and reaching 20 calls takes three awsf raise acts of five. This changes the character of the refusal. Measured over the eleven authored v2 ticket sets, the largest single milestone is six tickets, so MAX_CALL_CEILING was unreachable by a one-milestone shift and its test case (N = 31) was hypothetical. It is now reachable in ordinary use, which is why task 8 must tell the two refusals apart: this needs raise acts and no raise can fund this are different sentences to read at a terminal before leaving the house.

The operational consequence for markers

A multi-milestone shift, when it lands, flips every selected milestone's markers and every selected ticket's state: in one commit, and its single review covers the accumulated diff across all of them. That is the difference the owner will actually notice, and it is consistent with AGENTS.md invariant 2 — the markers are earned by the work that landed in that commit.
