AWSF

Driving the factory

One file. Open it anywhere, with nothing installed and no network.

You do not type the commands in this guide. You describe the job in your own words, and marimba — the assistant that drives the factory — runs them for you. They are shown so you can see what it did on your behalf. Two things are yours to do personally, and the guide says so when it reaches them: installing the repository, and the act at the end that only a person may perform.

Orientation

Start here

What this is, who does what, and what it costs before you begin.

This is one route through a factory that turns a job you describe in ordinary words into a change waiting for a person to sign for it. Six legs, in the order you travel them. Everything on the route is something you ask for. Almost nothing on it is something you type.

Who does what

  • Youdescribe the job you want and the boundary that decides whether it is done, then read what comes back and decide what happens to it.
  • marimbais the assistant you talk to. It picks and runs the commands, watches the work, and explains what came back. It never performs the act at the end.
  • The factorydoes the work in a sealed copy of the project, records everything it did, runs the project’s own checks against the result, and then stops in front of a person.

What this costs, before you start rather than after

Leg 3 spends real money and the other five legs are free. When you ask for a job, a paid provider is called, and that call is spent whether or not you like what comes back. The worked example on leg 3 should be one call — one job, one attempt, and no second opinion bought on top of it.

There is no free rehearsal, and that was measured rather than assumed. The repository’s README.md describes a route that spends nothing, and the route is real — but a job you drive cannot reach it. Somebody ran that experiment on a real drive before this guide was written, which is why the sentence is on the first page instead of being discovered by you at leg 3 with the money already gone. If you want to practise for nothing, practise the other five legs: installing, priming, watching, reading a refusal and stopping at the signature all cost nothing.

Nothing on this route lands anything. The route ends with the work waiting for a signature that this guide never tells you to give, so the checkout you start from is the checkout you finish with. That is what lets the worked example be run again, by you and by whoever reads this after you.

The other half of this file

Everything from Commands onward is the same factory described from the other side: what the assistant actually invoked, what the stages and moves are called, what only a person may do, and what a run is allowed to spend. It is there for when you want to look rather than to be read first. Each leg below ends with a short note pointing into the part of it that belongs to that leg.

Walkthrough · leg 1 of 6

Install it

The one leg you perform yourself, at a terminal, before any assistant is involved.

You are aiming at one thing: a copy of the repository on your machine that passes its own checks. Not a configured project and not a running job — a checkout that works.

The repository’s own README.md owns this leg, and this guide deliberately does not repeat it. Follow its Install and usage section, then its Verifying these claims section, in that order. The second one is the part people skip and the part that matters: it is how you find out the install worked instead of assuming it did.

The reason for pointing rather than copying is the same reason this whole document exists. A second copy of an install sequence goes stale quietly, and a stale install sequence sends you down a path the repository no longer takes.

You are done with leg 1 when the repository’s verification finishes without complaint. If it does not, stop here. Every later leg assumes it did, and a factory driven from a broken checkout produces confusing refusals instead of obvious ones.

Two more things have to be installed before leg 2, and neither of them is in the README.md, because neither of them lives in the repository. Leg 2 names them. If somebody handed you this file, that is the point where you may have to go back to them.

Behind the curtain None of this is the factory’s business yet: no job exists and it records nothing. The one thing that carries forward is that the checks pass, because the factory later runs the project’s own configured checks against whatever it produces. Which checks a project has configured is in Workflows and gates.

Walkthrough · leg 2 of 6

Prime a session

Opening an assistant that already knows the factory, and getting it to stop.

No command in this repository opens a driving session. A marimba session is opened by a launcher installed on your machine: it starts an assistant with marimba’s own settings file and appends the driving contract to that assistant’s instructions. The launcher and the settings are per-person and per-machine. Neither is in the checkout you just installed, so nothing you run from it will produce one.

This guide cannot tell you how to install them, because they live outside the repository and differ from machine to machine. If you do not already have both working, leg 2 cannot start, and the person who handed you this file is who to ask. Everything from here on assumes a session that opened with marimba’s settings and contract in place.

Once a session is open, prime it. In a session that has the command installed, /prime-awsf is the whole instruction; otherwise ask for it in words:

Ask for“Prime this session for driving the factory, then stop.”

Priming reads the sources that govern the factory, in a fixed order, and then runs a toolchain check — the repository’s own install-and-verify sequence from leg 1, plus the factory’s self-diagnosis:

npm run awsf -- doctor

You may see that same command written three ways — as an npm script, as a just target, or as a bare awsf command. They are three spellings of one thing rather than three commands, and Commands is the list of names that actually exist.

How to read what comes back

A healthy report, and then silence. A primed session should not volunteer a status board, and if yours does, the problem is with the session rather than with you: state printed before you have asked for anything was stale before you read it. Nothing about your job exists at this point, because you have not described one yet.

Behind the curtain Priming reads; it does not act. The self-diagnosis it runs is read-only and has no repair path, so a finding is evidence for you and never permission for the assistant to change something. It is one of the names in Commands.

Walkthrough · leg 3 of 6

Give it one small job

The worked example, the choice you will be asked to make, and the only leg that spends anything.

What you bring is a result and a boundary — not a command, and not a ticket number. Describe what you want to be true when the job is finished, and describe how anyone would tell that it is. You do not pick a command, you do not need a ticket to point at, and you do not have to know which part of the factory will do it. Choosing the command is marimba’s job, and it is the reason the route has an assistant on it at all.

The worked example

Ask for“The repository has a justfile whose targets are meant to be thin wrappers over the project’s npm scripts. Add one test that checks that promise: every target that delegates to an npm script must name a script that actually exists. Put it with the other tests that check documents and configuration against the code. Do not change the justfile and do not change any existing test. It is done when the new test passes and the whole suite still passes.”

That is a paragraph of English with a boundary at the end of it. It names a result, the place the result belongs, two things not to touch, and the condition that decides it is finished.

Why an example and not a real job. This one can be run again — by you, tomorrow, and by the next person who reads this file — because the route never lands anything, so the checkout is unchanged afterwards and the job is still missing when the next person asks for it. A real job is only new once, which makes it useless as something to practise on.

Four things that surprise people here

The six legs are the order you travel, not an order the factory enforces. If you decided what you wanted before you opened a session — most people do — nothing is wrong and nothing needs redoing. The factory checks the shape of what it is handed, never the order in which you thought of it.

Before anything launches, you will be asked to choose. marimba does not start work on a description. It comes back with the ways it could proceed and waits for you. Expect three:

  • Drive it through the factoryThe whole route: registered, sealed, run, checked, and stopped in front of you. This is the one this guide describes, and the one that spends a call.
  • Prepare it without launchingThe job is registered and everything is made ready, and it stops before anything is spent. Useful when you want to see what would happen; it is not this route.
  • Hand it straight to a workerThe older convention, which skips the factory. Nothing is recorded, no checks are run against the result, and there is nothing waiting for a signature at the end. If you pick this one you are not driving the factory, you are just using an assistant.

The thing that does the work may not be the assistant you are talking to. The worker is a configured role with its own provider, chosen by the project’s configuration rather than by your session, so a session running on one provider can hand the job to a worker running on another. That is normal rather than a fault, and it is settled by configuration rather than by conversation.

The work happens in a sealed copy. Nothing in your checkout changes while the job runs. If the job goes wrong, the sealed copy is what goes wrong.

What marimba runs, once you have chosen

npm run awsf -- new TASK "<the paragraph you wrote>"
npm run awsf -- start TASK
npm run awsf -- run TASK

Three commands in that order: the job is registered, a sealed copy of the project is made ready, and the work begins — and it is that third step, not the second, where a provider call is reserved and spent. Stopping after the second one costs nothing. marimba also passes options that pick the route and the risk setting; those belong to the other half of this file rather than to anything you have to decide. TASK stands for whatever name your job is given — you will see the real one in the session.

Behind the curtain Those three are names in Commands, and between them the job makes the first of the recorded moves in Legal edges. The reservation made at the third step is what Risk tiers and call ceilings governs: a run cannot quietly spend more than the job was allowed.

Walkthrough · leg 4 of 6

Watch it work

Which surface answers which question, so you stop alternating between two of them.

Watch the dashboard; ask marimba. They are not two views of the same thing, and flipping between them is the most reliable way to spend a run learning nothing. The dashboard answers what is happening. marimba answers why, and what happens next.

npm run awsf -- dash
npm run awsf -- status TASK

The first serves the dashboard. The second is the same story as text, in the session, if you would rather not leave it.

Where each question is answered

  • Has it started, and where has it got to?The job’s card on the dashboard. This is the question the dashboard is best at, and the one you should stop asking marimba.
  • What was the factory actually asked to do?The request row’s evidence. This is the request as the factory received it, which is not always the request as you said it — leg 6 is where that distinction earns its own row.
  • What did the worker do, and what did it produce?The builder row’s evidence and its events. The work itself is readable there, as it happens, rather than summarised afterwards.
  • Did the project’s checks run, and did they pass?The checks row — with the caution below.
  • Why is it doing that, and what should I do about it?marimba. Anything that needs a judgement rather than a value is a question for the session.

Read the checks row exactly. When it passes, it says that all configured commands passed. That is a statement about the list of checks this project has configured — and if that list is empty, the same green sentence appears with nothing at all behind it. It is not a claim that your code is good; it is a claim that a list ran and nothing in it failed. Which checks a project has configured is in Workflows and gates, and that is the thing to check rather than the sentence.

Behind the curtain Everything on this leg is read-only: watching a job changes nothing about it. While you watch, the job passes through several recorded stages, and every move between them is recorded rather than inferred — the stages are in Lifecycle states and the moves in Legal edges.

Walkthrough · leg 5 of 6

Read a refusal

How to read a refusal you did not cause, and the one distinction everything else in this factory rests on.

A refusal is the factory declining to go on without something it requires. It is not a crash and it is not an error message: it is a recorded decision, with a reason attached, that leaves the job somewhere you can still act on it.

Read a refusal as the shape of what the factory will accept, not as a wall to get past. The boundaries are an operating envelope — they describe the conditions under which work goes forward. Nothing in this guide will ever tell you how to route around one, and if you find yourself looking for a way around, the thing to change is the request.

You may not get one, and you must not manufacture one. The factory refuses only when something it needs is genuinely missing, so a drive that goes well produces no refusal at all. Do not stage one to see what it looks like; a staged refusal is a fabricated record, and it teaches you the wrong thing about the real ones. Ask marimba to read a real one instead.

Ask for“Find a refusal that already happened and walk me through it. Read it only — change nothing.”

Earlier work stays visible, including work that was never yours. Durable records live in one place per machine, with each project kept separate inside it, so attempts from months ago are still there to be read. If marimba comes back with a job you do not recognise, it has not lost track of yours — it went and found a real refusal to show you. Reading one changes nothing about it.

A refusal is readable from the factory’s own records. What refused, what it required, and what it left available are all in what the factory writes as it goes: the job’s status, its running account of what happened, and the envelope of any opinion it asked for along the way. You should not need to open a plan document to understand one. If a question here can only be answered from a plan, that is a gap in this guide rather than something you did wrong.

npm run awsf -- status TASK

The four things to get out of it

  • What refusedWhich part of the factory stopped, and at which point of the job.
  • What it was protectingThe condition that was not met. This is the sentence worth reading twice; everything else follows from it.
  • What was not spentA refusal usually costs less than a success. Knowing what is still available decides what you can afford to try next.
  • What you may now decideA refused job is not a dead job. The factory names what remains open, and that list is what leg 6 hands you.

A worked refusal

Here is one of each of those four, with every name replaced by a placeholder. It is the shape of a refusal rather than a picture of one: the real thing carries your job’s own name and your job’s own numbers.

The story behind it is short. The work was built, the project’s configured checks ran and passed, and a second opinion was asked for. The opinion came back carrying a field the factory does not recognise. The factory will not record an opinion it cannot read in full, so the job arrived at the point where a valid opinion is required without one, and it stopped there.

Job TASK, in project PROJECT

  stopped at    the point where a second opinion is recorded
  what refused  the check that reads an opinion before recording it
  required      an opinion in the shape the factory accepts
  received      an opinion carrying one field the factory does not know
  spent         the calls the build itself used, before any of this
  not spent     nothing at all was spent on the refusal
  still open    the acts named on leg 6, and what is left to spend on them

Read what refused and required together and you have the whole refusal. Every other line is the situation around it. Notice what the refusal is not: nothing crashed, nothing was lost, and nothing about the job was undone. A condition the factory requires was not met, so it declined to go further and left everything where it was.

“What was not spent” is the line people skip, and it is the one that decides what you can afford next. Stopping is free. What was already spent getting to the stop is gone and does not come back, and it stays attached to the job rather than to the run — so asking for the same job again starts from what is left, not from the beginning. A second run started without knowing that number is a run that may not have enough left to finish.

A thing that stops you, and a record that reports on you

This is the distinction worth carrying away from this guide. Nearly everything in the factory is one of the two, they are easy to confuse in writing, and a reader who confuses them trusts the wrong one.

A lock stops you at the door. A camera above the door tells somebody afterwards that you went through. Both are worth having, neither does the other’s job, and no quantity of camera ever adds up to a lock.

  • Things that stopThe refusal you just read. The job did not go on. Something was actually prevented, at the moment it was attempted.
  • Things that reportThe dashboard, the status text, and the factory’s running account of what happened. They watched, and they wrote it down. They prevented nothing, and were never built to.

Where this bites in practice. The checks row on leg 4 is a report: a list ran, and nothing in it failed. That is a true and useful thing to be told. Read as though it had stopped something, it becomes a promise nobody made. The confusion runs the other way too — hunting through the records for the trace of something a stop prevented is hunting for a thing that, by design, never happened.

So when anyone tells you the factory will not let something happen — this guide included — the useful question is which of the two they mean. And a thing that stops, stops in one place. The refusal you read stopped that job at that point. It is not a claim that nothing anywhere could ever get past. It is the shape of what this factory accepts, which is what the top of this leg said, and it is why the move is always to change the request.

Behind the curtain A refusal names a condition, and the conditions are properties of the moves the factory is permitted to make; those moves are in Legal edges. The stage a refused job comes to rest in is one of the entries in Lifecycle states, and it is a resting place rather than a dead end.

Walkthrough · leg 6 of 6

Stop at the signature

The seven acts that are yours, and exactly what to have in hand before you choose one.

The route ends in front of you. The factory has done everything it is allowed to do and stopped, holding a candidate change that has not been merged into anything. This is the second and last thing you do personally.

Passing checks do not mean the job was done. They mean a list of checks ran against whatever was produced and nothing in it failed. Whether what was produced is what you asked for is a separate question, and it is yours. On the drive this guide was written from, a job arrived here with everything green and a candidate that had done a different thing from the one requested. Comparing the candidate against the boundary you wrote at leg 3 is part of the act, not a formality before it.

If the candidate does not match what you asked for, read the request the factory actually sent. It is recorded, and marimba can read it back to you. A mismatch is usually visible there in plain words — a clause dropped, or a constraint the request added on its own. What you should not have to do is read the source code to find out why; if you do, that is a gap in this guide, and it is worth saying so.

Ask for“Show me the request as the factory received it, and how the candidate compares with the boundary I gave you.”

Nothing expires while you decide. A candidate waiting for a person stays waiting. Closing the session, shutting the machine, or coming back tomorrow changes nothing about the job’s recorded state — the session was the thing driving, never the thing holding. And a candidate you never sign for changes nothing at all, which is exactly what makes the worked example safe for the next person to run.

The seven acts that are yours

Seven acts can move a job from here, and every one of them is yours: land, cancel, rework, review, journey, raise and publish. What makes them one set is not what they do, because they do quite different things. It is who may do them. Each is a decision a person takes, and each is refused to an assistant.

Refused is the exact word, and this is the place to use leg 5’s distinction on something that matters. The refusal is a thing that stops, not a record that reports: the surface marimba runs through denies the call before the act can begin. So it holds whether or not the assistant has read any instruction, agrees with it, or has been talked round to a different view. What you will see instead is marimba preparing the act, explaining it, and stopping. That stop is the machine, not the manners.

Each of the seven shows you what it is about to do and then asks. That screen is the authority on what the act costs and what it settles — for your job, at that moment, worked out there rather than copied from anywhere, this guide included. Read it before you answer it. What each of the seven is for is in Owner acts.

npm run awsf -- status TASK
npm run awsf -- land TASK

The first is the evidence. The second is the act, and you type it yourself. TASK stands for whatever name your job was given; you will see the real one in the session. land is shown here because it is the act this route most often ends at, not because it is the one to pick. marimba will prepare and explain whichever of the seven the evidence supports, and it is denied all seven equally.

What must be in hand before you decide

marimba’s whole job on this leg is to put a fixed set of things in front of you. The set is short and it is the same every time. If one of them is missing, that missing thing is what to ask for.

  • The handleWhich job this is, and which stage it has come to rest in.
  • What was asked forThe request as the factory received it — not as you said it. Leg 4 is where that gap first becomes visible; this is the leg where it costs you something.
  • What came backThe candidate change, set beside the boundary you wrote at leg 3.
  • Claim and measurement, kept apartWhat the worker said it did, and what the checks measured, as two separate statements. The first is not evidence for the second, and where they disagree, the measurement is the one that is right.
  • What is leftWhat the job may still spend, how many correction rounds remain, and what is left of the allowance for sending it back for another look. Your job’s numbers, not general ones.
  • Which acts the evidence supportsOf the seven, which ones the evidence would carry, which ones it would not, and the reason for each.

This guide stops here, and the stop is deliberate. It tells you what to have in hand before you decide. It does not tell you which way to decide, and it never will.

Behind the curtain The acts only a person may perform are the list in Owner acts, and each one makes a recorded move of its own in Legal edges. That an assistant is refused them is a property of the tool surface it runs under, which is why it holds whether or not the assistant has read anything.

Boundaries

What you are never asked to do

These are rules, not gaps for you or marimba to fill.

  • Push anythingNever push a branch or publish a change, because this route ends before the human decision and publication is a separate human-initiated act.
  • Edit a protected fileNever edit one yourself or ask marimba to edit one, because those files define the factory’s policy and state boundaries and require their own owner-approved change.
  • Repair state by handNever alter a journal, status record, lifecycle, reservation, or Git state to make a job move, because a record changed outside the factory is evidence the factory did not create.
  • Work around a refusalNever bypass, retry around, or manufacture a refusal, because the refusal names a condition the factory requires and a detour would fabricate the evidence it is meant to protect.
  • Open private/Never open it, because it contains host-private continuity material and prompts; naming the boundary makes it a rule rather than relying on habit to avoid an unmentioned directory.

Boundaries

What this document owns

The reader’s route and six fenced fact classes, and nothing else.

This file owns the six-leg route you have just read and its six reference classes: commands, lifecycle states, legal edges, owner acts, workflows and gates, and risk tiers and call ceilings. Each class is held equal to its source, so this file may describe those sets without becoming their owner.

The sources own the rest: README.md owns installation and repository verification; docs/driving/skills/awsf/SKILL.md owns how a driving session operates; core/src/ owns factory behaviour; awsf.config.yaml owns the configured route and checks; and the source files named beside each reference class own its exact facts. This guide points to them. It does not replace, configure, or change them.

Nothing in the factory reads this document. No gate, transition, guard, reservation, accounting decision, or outcome depends on anybody having read it. If the factory needs this guide to be safe or correct, the factory has a defect that belongs in its code.

Boundaries

What this document does not cover

Known limits, named with the person or workstream that owns each next step.

  • The W12 observation protocolThis guide does not explain the ticket set or its recording questions, because specs/tickets/awsf-v2-w12-cheatsheet/README.md owns that protocol; a later drive that needs it to proceed is a documented gap.
  • A refusal from your current jobThis guide cannot promise one, because a successful job has no real refusal; read an existing refusal without changing it until an M5 graduation run supplies a genuine one.
  • The order or numbering in a session reportThe six legs are this guide’s observation order, not a factory sequence; if a session’s numbering makes the graduation record unreadable, W01 owns the contract change.
  • Exact session-specific invocations outside the six classesThis guide does not list task-request, Git-formatting, or state-store read arguments, because no fenced source owns a complete set; if the route needs one, it reopens as a document gap rather than becoming an unfenced list.
  • Journal size or compositionThis guide sends you to the dashboard rather than a journal, and no current workstream owns changing what the journal stores.
  • A new project’s entry pathThis route is for this checkout, because preparing a greenfield project currently requires protected configuration; W03 owns that missing entry path.
  • Installing marimba or its launcherThose are per-person, per-machine setup owned by the person who hands you this file, not by this repository or this guide.

What this has been shown to prove

This guide has been driven by its author and not yet by anyone else. It is known to be sufficient to drive with; it is not known to be understandable to somebody new. Only a second reader can establish that, and this document does not claim it has happened.
